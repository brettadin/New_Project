# 3.10 — Scientific Visualization and Presentation

## Purpose

The application should provide comprehensive scientific visualization capabilities for examining, comparing, interpreting and communicating astronomical data, analytical outputs, models and research results.

Visualization is an essential part of scientific investigation. Researchers need to examine numerical measurements, identify patterns, investigate features, compare observations, evaluate model predictions and communicate scientific findings.

Visualization should therefore be treated as an integrated scientific capability rather than simply a method of displaying completed analytical results.

The application should provide a shared visualization foundation that can be used across different scientific disciplines while accommodating the specialized requirements of individual data types and research methods.

Visualization capabilities should support both exploratory investigation and scientific presentation, preserving the accuracy, context, uncertainty and provenance of the information being displayed.

The scientific requirements for visualization are established in this subsection. The associated user experience, interaction behaviour, customization and presentation controls are addressed in Section 6.7.

## 3.10.1 General Scientific Visualization Framework

### Core Requirements

- Provide shared scientific visualization capabilities accessible from relevant scientific workspaces.
- Support visualization of observational data, processed measurements, derived results, scientific models and simulations.
- Accommodate different scientific data types, dimensions and measurement conventions.
- Support both general-purpose and discipline-specific visualization methods.
- Allow visualizations to be generated from relevant datasets and analytical outputs.
- Preserve relationships between visualized information and its underlying scientific resources.
- Support multiple representations of the same dataset where scientifically appropriate.
- Distinguish observations, processed measurements, derived results, model predictions and simulated information.
- Preserve appropriate units, labels, scales, uncertainty and scientific context.
- Accommodate additional visualization methods as scientific capabilities expand.

### Design Guidance

The application should establish a shared visualization foundation rather than developing completely independent presentation systems for every scientific discipline.

However, shared infrastructure should not force fundamentally different data types into inappropriate visualization formats.

For example, spectroscopy, astronomical imaging, orbital dynamics and statistical population analysis have different visualization requirements. Their tools should remain specialized where necessary while benefiting from common interaction, presentation and configuration capabilities.

## 3.10.2 Scientific Graphing and Plotting

### Core Requirements

Allow users to create and examine scientific graphs and plots for different types of astronomical data.

Potential graph types include:

- Spectral plots.
- Photometric light curves.
- Time-series graphs.
- Radial velocity curves.
- Transit and eclipse representations.
- Model-versus-observation comparisons.
- Statistical and analytical plots.
- Parameter relationships and correlations.
- Comparative planetary and stellar measurements.
- Population distributions.
- Other scientific graph types as additional capabilities are developed.

Support appropriate axis configurations, numerical scales, units, labels, legends and uncertainty representations.

Allow multiple scientific variables or datasets to be represented together where appropriate.

Support scientifically meaningful transformations and representations, including logarithmic scales, normalization and other appropriate plotting conventions.

Preserve the relationship between plotted measurements and their original datasets.

### Design Guidance

Graphing should be sufficiently flexible to accommodate different scientific requirements without restricting users to a small set of predefined plot types.

Specialized graphing capabilities should remain available where individual disciplines require particular representations or analytical conventions.

## 3.10.3 Multi-Dataset and Comparative Visualization

### Core Requirements

Support visualization of multiple datasets within a shared scientific view where appropriate.

Relevant capabilities include:

- Overlaying multiple spectra.
- Comparing observations from different instruments.
- Comparing observations obtained at different times.
- Displaying reference spectra alongside astronomical observations.
- Comparing observed and modelled spectra.
- Comparing multiple light curves.
- Comparing planetary and stellar measurements.
- Displaying alternative model predictions.
- Comparing results from different analytical methods.
- Presenting multiple related scientific datasets in coordinated views.

Allow users to distinguish individual datasets through appropriate visual properties, labels and legends.

Support combined overlays, separate panels, synchronized views and other appropriate comparison arrangements.

Preserve the identity and source information of each contributing dataset.

Avoid silently transforming datasets to create the appearance of direct comparability.

### Design Guidance

Comparative visualization should support meaningful scientific investigation rather than merely displaying multiple datasets together.

Differences in units, wavelength coverage, resolution, calibration, measurement conventions and uncertainty may affect how datasets should be presented.

The application should communicate important compatibility limitations and distinguish direct comparisons from representations requiring scientific preparation.

Detailed requirements for scientific comparison and data integration are established in Section 3.9.

## 3.10.4 Spectral Visualization

### Core Requirements

Support visualization of astronomical, planetary, stellar and laboratory spectra.

Relevant capabilities include:

- Intensity and flux spectra.
- Transmission and emission spectra.
- Absorption and emission features.
- Spectral line measurements.
- Molecular and atomic reference spectra.
- Multiple spectral overlays.
- Observed-versus-modelled comparisons.
- Spectral residuals and differences.
- Uncertainty representations.
- Continuum and normalization comparisons.
- Spectral regions of interest.
- Different wavelength, frequency and wavenumber representations.

Allow appropriate treatment of spectral units, axis conventions, resolution and observational context.

Support comparison between astronomical spectra and relevant laboratory measurements, reference databases and synthetic model outputs.

Preserve the distinction between measured spectral information, processed spectra and theoretical predictions.

### Design Guidance

Spectral visualization should support both broad examination of entire spectra and detailed investigation of individual features.

The application should accommodate different spectral representations and units without assuming that all spectra share the same axis conventions or measurement characteristics.

Spectral visualization should integrate with the analysis capabilities established in Section 3.3 and the atmospheric modelling requirements in Section 3.7.

## 3.10.5 Photometric and Time-Series Visualization

### Core Requirements

Support visualization of photometric measurements and time-dependent astronomical observations.

Relevant capabilities include:

- Stellar and planetary light curves.
- Transit and eclipse light curves.
- Phase-folded light curves.
- Periodic and irregular variability.
- Radial velocity time series.
- Multi-epoch observations.
- Long-term monitoring.
- Time-dependent model predictions.
- Observed-versus-modelled comparisons.
- Residual and difference representations.
- Measurement uncertainties.
- Relevant event markers and timing information.

Allow appropriate time axes, reference epochs, time systems and measurement conventions.

Support comparison of multiple time-series datasets where scientifically appropriate.

Preserve the relationship between plotted events and their underlying observational records.

### Design Guidance

Time-series visualization should allow users to examine both individual measurements and broader temporal behaviour.

For example, users should be able to examine transit shapes, compare repeated observations, investigate variability and evaluate model predictions against observed light curves.

Phase-folded representations and other transformed views should remain distinguishable from the original chronological observations.

These capabilities should integrate with the photometric and time-series analysis requirements established in Section 3.4.

## 3.10.6 Astronomical Image Visualization

### Core Requirements

Support scientific visualization of astronomical images and image-derived information.

Relevant capabilities include:

- Two-dimensional astronomical image display.
- Multi-extension and multidimensional image representations where appropriate.
- Image scaling and display transformations.
- Brightness and contrast adjustment.
- Colour mapping and intensity representations.
- Coordinate grids and spatial reference information.
- Image overlays and annotations.
- Multi-image comparisons.
- Multi-wavelength image presentations.
- Image difference and residual representations.
- Spatial measurement and source-identification overlays.

Support appropriate astronomical image formats and relevant coordinate information.

Preserve the distinction between the underlying image data and transformations applied solely for display.

Allow visualization of image measurements and associated analytical results where appropriate.

### Design Guidance

Astronomical image visualization should provide meaningful ways to examine both the original image data and its scientifically appropriate display representations.

Brightness, contrast, colour mapping and scaling may change how information appears without changing the underlying measurements.

The application should make this distinction clear.

Detailed image processing and spatial analysis requirements are established in Section 3.5.

## 3.10.7 Spatial and Coordinate-Based Visualization

### Core Requirements

Support visualization of astronomical information involving spatial positions, coordinate systems and geometric relationships.

Relevant capabilities include:

- Celestial coordinate representations.
- Spatial positions and distributions.
- Angular separation and relative positioning.
- Source and object locations.
- Coordinate grids and reference frames.
- Spatial measurement overlays.
- Relationships between astronomical objects.
- Spatially resolved observations.
- Appropriate projections and coordinate transformations.
- Comparison of spatial information from different sources.

Preserve the coordinate system, reference frame, units and relevant conventions associated with displayed information.

Communicate limitations introduced by projections, transformations and incomplete spatial coverage.

### Design Guidance

Spatial visualization should support appropriate representation of astronomical positions and relationships without obscuring the underlying coordinate conventions.

Different astronomical datasets may use different coordinate systems, reference frames and projections.

The application should preserve these distinctions and avoid implying that visually aligned positions are directly equivalent when coordinate transformations or uncertainties remain unresolved.

## 3.10.8 Orbital, Planetary and Dynamical Visualization

### Core Requirements

Support visualization of planetary systems, orbital configurations and relevant dynamical information.

Relevant representations include:

- Individual orbital paths.
- Multi-planet system configurations.
- Relative planetary positions.
- Orbital orientation and inclination.
- Orbital motion over time.
- Transit and eclipse geometry.
- Relative positions of planets and host stars.
- Orbital phase and event timing.
- Gravitationally interacting systems where appropriate.
- Comparative planetary system configurations.
- Model-generated trajectories and dynamical simulations.

Support two-dimensional and three-dimensional representations where scientifically appropriate.

Allow visualization of relevant orbital parameters and model outputs alongside their graphical representations.

Support interactive examination of alternative orbital configurations and time-dependent positions where appropriate.

### Design Guidance

Orbital visualizations should preserve meaningful relationships between orbital parameters and displayed geometry.

The application should distinguish scientifically scaled representations from simplified explanatory diagrams and illustrative presentations.

Where visualizations use simplified distances, dimensions, timescales or motion, those limitations should be communicated.

Orbital calculations and dynamical analysis are established in Section 3.8. This subsection establishes the corresponding visualization requirements.

## 3.10.9 Model and Simulation Visualization

### Core Requirements

Support visualization of scientific model outputs, simulations and predicted physical properties.

Relevant representations include:

- Model-generated spectra.
- Observed-versus-modelled comparisons.
- Atmospheric profiles.
- Temperature-pressure distributions.
- Molecular abundance profiles.
- Model parameter relationships.
- Simulation outputs over time.
- Predicted physical quantities.
- Parameter sensitivity representations.
- Alternative model configurations.
- Model residuals and differences.
- Uncertainty and confidence representations where appropriate.

Allow users to examine model outputs alongside relevant input observations and parameters.

Support comparison of different model configurations and assumptions.

Preserve the distinction between model predictions, simulated information and direct observations.

### Design Guidance

Model visualization should help users understand how a scientific model represents physical processes and how its predictions relate to available observations.

For example, atmospheric modelling may produce temperature-pressure profiles, molecular abundance distributions and synthetic spectra.

These outputs should be distinguishable from measured atmospheric properties and should retain their association with the model configuration that generated them.

The general modelling requirements are established in Section 3.7.

## 3.10.10 Statistical and Analytical Visualization

### Core Requirements

Support visualization of scientific measurements, analytical relationships and statistical results.

Potential representations include:

- Histograms.
- Scatter plots.
- Distribution plots.
- Correlation plots.
- Regression representations.
- Residual plots.
- Error and uncertainty distributions.
- Parameter-space visualizations.
- Population comparisons.
- Statistical summaries.
- Other appropriate analytical representations.

Allow visualization of relevant statistical quantities, uncertainty and sample characteristics.

Support comparison between alternative analytical methods and datasets.

Preserve the underlying measurements and methods used to produce statistical outputs.

### Design Guidance

Statistical visualization should help users examine patterns, relationships, distributions and potential anomalies.

Visual presentation should not obscure sample size, selection criteria, uncertainty or other limitations that affect interpretation.

Statistical relationships should not be presented as establishing causation without appropriate scientific evidence.

These capabilities should integrate with the comparative and population-level analysis requirements established in Section 3.9.

### Core Requirements

Allow visualization capabilities to expand beyond conventional two-dimensional scientific graphs.

Potential areas include:

- Three-dimensional scientific representations.
- Multidimensional datasets.
- Spatially resolved spectral information.
- Time-dependent spatial data.
- Interactive parameter-space exploration.
- Combined spatial and temporal representations.
- Specialized planetary and astronomical visualizations.
- Other discipline-specific visualization methods.

Support appropriate methods for navigating and inspecting complex scientific representations.

Preserve relevant dimensions, units, coordinate systems and data relationships.

Ensure specialized visualizations remain connected to their underlying scientific data and analytical context.

### Design Guidance

Advanced multidimensional visualization can provide useful insight but may introduce substantial interaction, performance and usability requirements.

The application should introduce these capabilities when they provide meaningful scientific value.

Conventional graphs, tables and image representations should remain available alongside more advanced visualizations.

## 3.10.12 Uncertainty, Quality and Scientific Context

### Core Requirements

Scientific visualizations should communicate relevant information about data quality, uncertainty and observational limitations.

Support appropriate representations of:

- Measurement uncertainties.
- Error bars.
- Confidence intervals.
- Model uncertainty.
- Residuals.
- Missing or unavailable measurements.
- Flagged or excluded observations.
- Data quality indicators.
- Relevant calibration or processing limitations.
- Incomplete observational coverage.

Allow users to examine the scientific context associated with displayed information.

Preserve the distinction between measured values, estimates, derived quantities and model-dependent predictions.

Avoid visual presentation choices that imply unsupported precision or certainty.

### Design Guidance

Scientific visualization should not make uncertain or incomplete information appear more reliable than the underlying evidence supports.

Uncertainty and data-quality information should be represented in ways appropriate to the scientific quantity and analytical method.

The application should allow users to inspect the underlying information rather than relying exclusively on visual indicators.

## 3.10.13 Scientific Annotations and Interpretation

### Core Requirements

Support the association of scientific annotations and contextual information with relevant visualizations.

Potential annotations include:

- Identified spectral features.
- Reference wavelengths or frequencies.
- Transit and eclipse markers.
- Relevant observational events.
- Measurement regions.
- Model parameters and reference values.
- Spatial object identifications.
- Significant measurements.
- User-generated scientific notes.
- Relevant literature or reference information.

Allow annotations to retain their relationship with the associated dataset, visualization or analysis.

Distinguish user interpretations from automatically generated information and established scientific identifications.

Preserve relevant annotation information when visualizations are saved, revisited or exported.

### Design Guidance

Annotations should help users record what they observe, identify relevant features and communicate their interpretations.

An annotation should not silently become part of the underlying scientific data.

The application should preserve the distinction between a measured feature, an automatically suggested identification and a user's interpretation.

Detailed interaction and annotation management requirements are addressed in Section 6.7.

## 3.10.14 Visualization Layouts and Presentation Arrangements

### Core Requirements

Support flexible arrangements of related scientific visualizations.

Potential arrangements include:

- Individual graphs and visualizations.
- Multiple graphs displayed together.
- Multi-panel scientific figures.
- Synchronized comparison panels.
- Graphs displayed alongside tables.
- Images displayed alongside analytical plots.
- Observations displayed alongside model predictions.
- Related visualizations from different scientific methods.
- Combined spatial, temporal and numerical representations where appropriate.

Allow related visualizations to retain their associations with relevant datasets and analyses.

Support arrangements that accommodate different scientific investigations without imposing one fixed layout on every research activity.

### Design Guidance

Some investigations benefit from a single detailed graph, while others require several related visualizations to examine the same object or phenomenon.

For example, an exoplanet investigation may benefit from displaying a transit light curve, an observed spectrum, a modelled spectrum and relevant atmospheric parameters together.

The application should support these arrangements while maintaining clear identification of the underlying data and the scientific meaning of each representation.

Detailed panel arrangement, layout customization and interaction requirements are established in Section 6.7 and coordinated with Section 6.8.

## 3.10.15 Saved Visualizations and Reusable Configurations

### Core Requirements

Allow users to save customized scientific visualizations and return to them later.

Saved visualization configurations should preserve, where technically feasible:

- Selected datasets and their references.
- Graph or visualization type.
- Layout and panel arrangement.
- Selected wavelength, time or spatial ranges.
- Axis settings, scales and units.
- Reference spectra and overlays.
- Display parameters.
- Labels, legends and annotations.
- Relevant uncertainty representations.
- Other visualization customization settings.

Allow users to:

- Reopen saved visualizations.
- Duplicate and modify existing configurations.
- Maintain multiple visualizations of the same dataset.
- Save reusable visualization configurations.
- Apply compatible configurations to other datasets.
- Preserve visualization configurations within relevant projects and research investigations.

The application should distinguish between saving a reproducible visualization configuration and exporting a static representation.

### Design Guidance

A saved visualization should preserve the information needed to reconstruct the previous scientific view, subject to the continued availability of its underlying resources and dependencies.

A saved configuration should not silently become scientifically misleading if its underlying data changes, becomes unavailable or is replaced by another version.

The application should identify relevant changes and communicate when a saved visualization can no longer be reproduced exactly.

Detailed preference management and visualization persistence requirements are coordinated with Sections 6.4, 6.7 and 6.8.

## 3.10.16 Export and Scientific Presentation

### Core Requirements

Allow users to export scientific visualizations for external examination, documentation and presentation.

Support appropriate export options, including:

- Common image formats.
- High-resolution image output.
- Vector graphics where appropriate.
- Publication-quality figures.
- Multi-panel figures.
- Export of related visualizations together.
- Export of underlying numerical data separately.
- Export of relevant visualization configurations where technically appropriate.

Preserve appropriate scientific labels, units, legends, annotations and uncertainty information.

Clearly identify whether exported material represents original observations, processed data, derived results, model outputs or simulated information.

Preserve relevant provenance and source information where appropriate.

### Design Guidance

Exporting a visualization should not be treated as equivalent to exporting the underlying scientific data.

Users should be able to obtain both a presentation-ready representation and the numerical or structured information needed for further scientific work.

Dedicated scientific reporting and broader research documentation are addressed elsewhere in the application requirements and should not be unnecessarily duplicated here.

## 3.10.17 Performance and Large-Data Visualization

### Core Requirements

- Support visualization of datasets with different sizes and complexities.
- Accommodate large scientific datasets without making routine exploration impractical.
- Investigate suitable approaches for efficient rendering and data access.
- Support appropriate data reduction or display optimization where necessary.
- Preserve the distinction between display optimization and modification of underlying measurements.
- Maintain scientifically meaningful representations when displaying large datasets.
- Communicate when visualization limitations require reduced detail or additional processing.
- Support specialized approaches for multidimensional and computationally intensive visualizations.
- Avoid unnecessary duplication of large datasets when creating multiple visual representations.

### Design Guidance

Astronomical observations can contain substantial amounts of numerical and spatial information.

Visualization methods should account for memory use, rendering performance, responsiveness and the complexity of the data being displayed.

Where the application uses downsampling, aggregation or other display optimizations, it should preserve access to the original measurements and communicate the nature of the displayed representation when scientifically relevant.

Performance optimization must not silently change the scientific data or imply that a reduced representation contains the full information of the original dataset.

## 3.10.18 Scientific Research and Technology Evaluation

### Core Requirements

The initial research and planning phase should investigate suitable approaches for implementing scientific visualization capabilities.

Research should consider:

- Established scientific plotting libraries.
- Interactive graphing frameworks.
- Astronomical image visualization tools.
- Two-dimensional and three-dimensional visualization approaches.
- Multidimensional scientific data visualization.
- Scientific annotation and interaction techniques.
- Visualization of large datasets.
- Performance and memory requirements.
- Export formats and publication-quality presentation.
- Reusable visualization configurations.
- Integration with scientific analysis and modelling tools.
- Compatibility with the broader application architecture.

Evaluate existing scientific visualization software and frameworks for their capabilities, limitations, licensing, dependencies and suitability for integration.

Consider whether a shared visualization foundation can accommodate the application's scientific requirements while allowing specialized tools to be introduced where necessary.

### Design Guidance

The application should benefit from established scientific visualization methods where appropriate rather than unnecessarily recreating mature capabilities.

Technology selection should be guided by scientific suitability, usability, performance, maintainability and integration requirements.

Specific implementation technologies should remain undecided until the relevant research and architectural evaluation have been completed.

## General Principles

Scientific visualization and presentation should:

- Provide shared visualization capabilities across scientific workspaces.
- Accommodate different astronomical data types and research disciplines.
- Support flexible scientific graphing and plotting.
- Allow meaningful multi-dataset and comparative visualization.
- Support specialized spectral, photometric, imaging, spatial, orbital and modelling representations.
- Accommodate statistical, analytical and multidimensional visualization.
- Preserve units, labels, metadata, uncertainty and scientific context.
- Distinguish observations, processed measurements, derived results, model predictions and simulations.
- Support scientific annotations without modifying underlying data.
- Allow users to save and revisit visualization configurations.
- Support appropriate export of visualizations and underlying scientific information.
- Remain responsive and scientifically meaningful when working with large datasets.
- Integrate with the scientific analysis, modelling and comparative capabilities established throughout Section 3.
- Remain extensible as additional scientific disciplines and methods are introduced.

## General Principle

Scientific visualization should be an integral part of astronomical research, allowing users to explore, inspect, compare, interpret and communicate scientific information while preserving its underlying data, context, uncertainty and meaning.
