# 8.8 — Scientific Review and Reproducibility

## Purpose

Establishes how the application community can evaluate, examine and independently reproduce scientific work shared through the platform.

The application should support different forms of scientific evaluation, including community feedback, practical testing, independent reproduction, scientific review and validation against appropriate evidence.

These activities should be distinguishable because they provide different levels and types of evidence about the reliability of scientific work.

The system should preserve review methods, supporting evidence, outcomes, limitations and reviewer contributions.

Scientific credibility should be based on documented evaluation rather than popularity, community ratings or unsupported claims of validity.

## 8.8.1 — Community Scientific Review

Users should be able to examine and provide structured feedback on scientific work shared through the application.

Reviewable material may include:

- Scientific investigations and research findings.
- Observational datasets and measurements.
- Spectroscopic and photometric analyses.
- Processing methods and analytical workflows.
- Mathematical and computational models.
- Scientific interpretations and conclusions.
- Reproducibility packages.
- User-created scientific tools and resources.

Reviews should allow participants to examine relevant scientific evidence, methodology, assumptions, limitations and supporting references.

The application should distinguish informal community feedback from more structured scientific evaluations.

Community members should be able to identify potential problems, suggest improvements, question assumptions and provide alternative interpretations.

Review participation should not automatically imply professional qualifications or scientific authority.

## 8.8.2 — Independent Reproduction of Research

The application should support independent attempts to reproduce scientific analyses and research findings.

Users should be able to obtain or access shared research material and attempt to reproduce the original work using its documented datasets, methods, parameters and configurations.

Potential reproduction activities include:

- Repeating spectral processing and analysis.
- Reproducing molecular identification procedures.
- Repeating transit fitting and photometric analysis.
- Reproducing atmospheric retrievals.
- Repeating statistical and numerical calculations.
- Recreating scientific models and simulations.
- Reproducing published or community-shared visualizations.
- Comparing independently generated results with original findings.

Reproduction records should preserve:

- Original investigation and results.
- Reproduction methodology.
- Input datasets and their versions.
- Software and dependency versions.
- Analytical configurations and parameters.
- Computational environment where relevant.
- Reproduced outputs.
- Differences and discrepancies.
- Possible explanations for differences.
- Reproduction conclusions and limitations.

The application should distinguish successful execution from successful reproduction.

An analysis completing without software errors does not establish that its scientific results have been reproduced.

Differences arising from datasets, software versions, numerical precision, computational environments or methodological assumptions should be documented.

A reproduction attempt should remain independently attributable to the researcher performing it.

## 8.8.3 — Scientific Method and Data Evaluation

Scientific review should allow examination of the underlying data and methods used in an investigation.

Potential evaluation areas include:

- Data origin and provenance.
- Observational quality and completeness.
- Instrument characteristics and limitations.
- Calibration and preprocessing.
- Data transformations.
- Analytical methodology.
- Mathematical and statistical procedures.
- Model assumptions and configurations.
- Uncertainty and error treatment.
- Supporting scientific references.
- Suitability of methods for the research objective.

Reviewers should be able to identify methodological concerns, unsupported assumptions, potential processing artifacts and limitations that could affect conclusions.

Evaluations should distinguish between technical correctness and scientific suitability.

A method may be implemented correctly but still be inappropriate for a particular dataset or research question.

The application should preserve relevant review findings and supporting evidence without automatically modifying the original investigation.

## 8.8.4 — Model and Result Validation

The application should support evaluation of scientific models, analytical implementations and resulting outputs against appropriate evidence.

Potential validation approaches include:

- Comparing results with established reference datasets.
- Testing methods against published benchmark cases.
- Comparing numerical implementations against analytical solutions where available.
- Comparing independent software implementations.
- Evaluating model predictions against observations.
- Examining residuals and discrepancies.
- Testing numerical stability and convergence.
- Evaluating sensitivity to assumptions and parameters.
- Comparing results with established scientific measurements.

Examples include:

- Evaluating spectral identification procedures against laboratory reference spectra.
- Testing transit-fitting implementations against known benchmark datasets.
- Comparing atmospheric retrieval results against published reference cases.
- Evaluating numerical methods against known analytical solutions.
- Comparing modelled spectra with appropriate observations.

Validation records should identify:

- What was evaluated.
- The validation method.
- Reference datasets or evidence used.
- Relevant configurations and conditions.
- Expected outcomes or comparison criteria.
- Actual results.
- Discrepancies and limitations.
- The resulting assessment.

Validation must be appropriate to the scientific method and intended application.

Agreement with a reference case may support confidence but does not independently establish universal correctness.

The application should communicate the scope and limitations of each validation rather than presenting a validated method as universally reliable.

## 8.8.5 — Review Documentation and Evidence

Scientific evaluations should preserve sufficient information to understand how conclusions were reached.

Relevant documentation may include:

- Reviewer comments and observations.
- Evaluation objectives and scope.
- Methods and criteria used.
- Datasets and references examined.
- Analytical configurations.
- Supporting calculations and comparisons.
- Identified problems and limitations.
- Recommendations and unresolved questions.
- Review outcomes.
- Dates and relevant version information.

Where practical, evaluations should link directly to the scientific material being examined.

Review documentation should remain distinguishable from the original researcher's conclusions and from other reviewers' assessments.

The application should preserve evaluation history when research is updated or revised.

Review records should not be silently replaced when subsequent assessments reach different conclusions.

## 8.8.6 — Review Status and Scientific Credibility

The application should distinguish between different forms and stages of scientific evaluation.

Potential classifications include:

**Community feedback**

Other users have commented on or discussed the research.

**Community tested**

Other users have attempted to use the methods, workflows, tools or configurations.

**Independently reproduced**

Another researcher has independently attempted to reproduce the original analysis or findings.

The outcome must be recorded separately, distinguishing successful, partially successful, unsuccessful and inconclusive reproduction attempts.

**Scientifically reviewed**

The scientific methodology, evidence, assumptions and conclusions have undergone a documented review.

**Scientifically validated**

The relevant method, implementation or results have been evaluated against appropriate reference evidence, benchmark cases or established scientific criteria.

These classifications should not automatically imply one another.

For example:

- Community feedback does not establish scientific validity.
- Community testing does not establish successful reproduction.
- Independent reproduction does not necessarily establish universal validity.
- Scientific review does not guarantee that every conclusion is correct.
- Validation against one reference case does not establish suitability for every application.

The application should make the actual evaluation status and its scope understandable to users.

Popularity, downloads, ratings and positive comments must never be treated as substitutes for scientific evidence.

## 8.8.7 — Reviewer Attribution and Contributions

The application should preserve attribution for users who contribute to scientific review, testing, reproduction and validation.

Relevant information may include:

- Reviewer or contributor identity.
- Public display name or pseudonym.
- Evaluation date.
- Contribution type.
- Material examined.
- Methods and references used.
- Evaluation outcomes.
- Subsequent corrections or updates.

Users should be able to distinguish original research contributions from independent evaluation and review contributions.

Reviewer qualifications, experience and institutional affiliations may be included where users choose to provide them.

However, self-reported credentials should not automatically be presented as independently verified.

Pseudonymous participation should remain supported in accordance with Section 8.2.

Reviewers should retain appropriate attribution for their contributions without being required to disclose their real identities publicly.

## 8.8.8 — Review Responses and Research Revisions

Researchers should be able to respond to feedback, reproduction attempts and scientific reviews concerning their work.

Potential functionality includes:

- Responding to reviewer comments.
- Providing additional explanations or references.
- Clarifying methodological decisions.
- Addressing identified limitations.
- Correcting errors.
- Revising analytical configurations.
- Producing updated results.
- Publishing revised versions of research material.

The application should preserve the relationship between the original investigation, review findings, researcher responses and subsequent revisions.

Significant changes should be identifiable and attributable.

Historical results should not be silently replaced by updated versions.

Where a revision affects previous findings, the application should communicate the relationship between the original and revised work.

Reviewers should be able to distinguish between the original material they examined and later modifications.

## 8.8.9 — Scientific Review Limitations and Transparency

The application should communicate the limitations and scope of scientific evaluations.

A review should not be presented as more comprehensive than the work actually performed.

For example:

- Reviewing a visualization does not establish that the underlying data is accurate.
- Reproducing a calculation does not necessarily validate the scientific assumptions.
- Testing a workflow on one dataset does not establish compatibility with all datasets.
- Validating a numerical implementation does not automatically validate every scientific interpretation produced by it.
- Community agreement does not establish scientific consensus.

The application should distinguish between what was examined, what evidence was available and what remains unverified.

Unresolved concerns and limitations should remain visible where relevant.

The absence of a review should not automatically imply that research is unreliable.

Unreviewed research should remain distinguishable from evaluated research without unnecessarily discouraging users from sharing preliminary findings.

## Future Possibilities

The following capabilities may be investigated as the application develops but are not initial implementation priorities.

### Scientific Credibility Records

The application may eventually maintain an evidence-based scientific credibility record for shared research, workflows, analytical tools and methods.

This should not be a single numerical rating.

Instead, it could preserve:

- Who performed each evaluation.
- What was examined.
- Which methods and criteria were used.
- Which datasets and references were consulted.
- Whether independent reproduction was attempted.
- Reproduction outcomes and discrepancies.
- Validation procedures and results.
- Identified limitations.
- Review dates and subsequent updates.

The record should distinguish between an evaluation being performed and the evidence actually supporting the material being evaluated.

It should also preserve the scope of each assessment rather than presenting a limited evaluation as comprehensive validation.

### Community Review Programmes

Potential developments include:

- Organized community review initiatives.
- Subject-specific scientific review groups.
- Expert reviewer networks.
- Structured review processes.
- Review requests and invitations.
- Advanced reviewer contribution recognition.

### Community Reproduction and Benchmarking

Potential developments include:

- Community reproduction challenges.
- Shared benchmark datasets.
- Independent comparison of analytical methods.
- Community-maintained reference cases.
- Reproducibility collections.
- Automated comparison against established reference results.

### Formal Scientific Review Integration

The application may eventually investigate integration with external scientific peer-review systems, research repositories and publication platforms.

This could support connections between application-based research evaluations and formal scientific publication processes.

Such integration should not imply that community review automatically constitutes formal journal peer review.

### Additional Future Capabilities

Other possibilities include:

- Advanced validation registries.
- Structured reviewer reports.
- Long-term review histories.
- Scientific review contribution recognition.
- Independent validation networks.
- Advanced evaluation search and discovery.
- Cross-investigation reproducibility comparisons.

These possibilities should be evaluated according to scientific value, feasibility and practical demand.

## Initial Implementation Priorities

The initial implementation should establish the foundations for transparent scientific evaluation without requiring a comprehensive peer-review platform.

Priority capabilities include:

- Distinguishing community feedback, community testing, independent reproduction, scientific review and validation.
- Supporting documentation of review methods and findings.
- Recording independent reproduction attempts and their outcomes.
- Preserving relevant datasets, configurations and references used during evaluations.
- Supporting basic evaluation of methods, models and analytical results.
- Recording identified limitations and discrepancies.
- Preserving reviewer attribution.
- Allowing researchers to respond to feedback and document revisions.
- Maintaining evaluation history.
- Communicating the actual scope and limitations of reviews.
- Ensuring popularity and community ratings are not presented as scientific validation.

The underlying system should accommodate future evidence-based scientific credibility records without requiring their complete implementation initially.

## Implementation Considerations

Scientific review and reproducibility should integrate with the application's existing scientific integrity, analysis reproducibility, validation, project management and community systems.

Particular attention should be given to:

- Preserving original research and historical results.
- Distinguishing different forms of scientific evaluation.
- Recording sufficient evidence to understand review outcomes.
- Maintaining independent attribution for reproduction attempts.
- Distinguishing technical execution from scientific reproduction.
- Communicating validation scope and limitations.
- Preserving review history through subsequent research revisions.
- Avoiding unsupported claims of scientific credibility.
- Ensuring review functionality does not replace established scientific validation procedures.

The application should establish a reliable foundation for community evaluation while allowing more advanced review and validation capabilities to develop over time.

**General Principle:** Scientific review and reproducibility should provide transparent, evidence-based ways to examine research, independently reproduce results and evaluate scientific methods. The application should preserve the actual procedures, evidence, outcomes and limitations of each evaluation, distinguishing community feedback from independent reproduction, scientific review and validation. Scientific credibility should be supported by documented evidence rather than popularity, agreement or unsupported claims of reliability.
