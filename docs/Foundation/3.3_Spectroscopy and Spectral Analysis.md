

# 3.3 — Spectroscopy and Spectral Analysis

## Purpose

The application should provide comprehensive spectroscopy and spectral-analysis capabilities for investigating the physical and chemical properties of astronomical objects.

Spectroscopy should be a major scientific capability within the application, supporting research across exoplanetary and planetary atmospheres, stellar astronomy, solar observations and other relevant astronomical disciplines.

The application should support the complete analytical process of working with astronomical spectra: examining observational measurements, preparing and processing spectral data, identifying and characterizing spectral features, comparing observations with scientific reference information, and evaluating possible physical and chemical interpretations.

Capabilities should accommodate different spectral representations, wavelength regions, instruments, observational techniques and levels of analytical complexity.

Spectral analysis should support both exploratory investigation and advanced quantitative research while preserving the distinction between observations, derived measurements, model outputs and scientific interpretations.

## 3.3.1 Spectral Data Representation and Examination

### Core Requirements

- Support different spectral representations, including wavelength, frequency and wavenumber.
- Accommodate different wavelength regions and spectral coverage.
- Preserve the distinction between spectral-axis values and measured signal values.
- Support different signal types, including flux, intensity, transmittance, absorbance and other relevant measurements.
- Display available measurement units and axis conventions.
- Allow users to inspect individual spectral measurements and selected regions.
- Support examination of multiple spectra within a single investigation.
- Preserve relevant metadata and observational context.
- Accommodate spectra with different sampling intervals, spectral resolutions and measurement conventions.
- Identify incomplete spectral coverage and gaps in available measurements.

### Design Guidance

Spectral data should not be restricted to a single axis convention or measurement type.

Different instruments and research disciplines may represent spectra differently. The application should interpret these representations according to their scientific meaning rather than assuming that all spectral datasets contain equivalent quantities.

Users should be able to understand what is being plotted, which units are used and what physical quantity the signal represents.

## 3.3.2 Spectral Visualization and Interactive Examination

### Core Requirements

- Provide clear and scientifically appropriate spectral plots.
- Allow users to examine full spectral ranges and selected regions.
- Support zooming, panning and inspection of individual measurements.
- Allow multiple spectra to be displayed together where appropriate.
- Support overlaid and vertically offset spectral comparisons.
- Provide appropriate axis labels, units and legends.
- Allow relevant spectral features and measurement regions to be marked or annotated.
- Support customizable presentation settings without altering underlying measurements.
- Preserve the distinction between measured data and visual enhancements.
- Support export of spectral visualizations and associated information.
- Allow different wavelength regions to be examined individually or alongside one another.

### Design Guidance

Spectral visualization should support both rapid examination and detailed investigation.

Users may need to inspect an entire spectrum to understand its overall structure or focus on a narrow region to investigate individual spectral features.

Visual presentation options should not silently modify the scientific data. Any transformations that affect measurements must remain distinguishable from purely visual adjustments.

Detailed application-wide visualization and presentation requirements are established in Section 6.7, while the scientific visualization capabilities are further developed in Section 3.10.

## 3.3.3 Spectral Calibration and Corrections

### Core Requirements

Support appropriate spectral calibration and correction procedures, including where scientifically applicable:

- Wavelength or frequency calibration.
- Wavenumber calibration.
- Wavelength-dependent instrumental response correction.
- Flux calibration.
- Atmospheric and telluric correction.
- Background and baseline correction.
- Instrumental line-spread and resolution-related corrections.
- Redshift and radial-velocity corrections.
- Other instrument-specific or observation-specific corrections.

For applicable procedures:

- Identify required inputs and assumptions.
- Allow users to inspect relevant parameters.
- Preserve original spectral measurements.
- Create identifiable derived outputs.
- Record the corrections and transformations applied.
- Communicate when required calibration information is unavailable.
- Distinguish between calibrated observations and uncalibrated measurements.
- Identify when available calibration information is incomplete or unsuitable for a particular operation.

### Design Guidance

Calibration and correction procedures must reflect the instrument, observation type and intended scientific analysis.

Not every spectrum requires the same corrections, and some procedures may be inappropriate for particular datasets.

The application should avoid treating calibration as a universal automatic operation. Users should be able to understand which corrections have been applied and what information supports them.

## 3.3.4 Continuum Examination and Normalization

### Core Requirements

- Support examination of spectral continua.
- Allow users to identify or select continuum regions.
- Support appropriate continuum estimation methods.
- Provide spectral normalization capabilities.
- Allow users to review relevant normalization settings.
- Preserve the original spectrum.
- Distinguish normalized spectra from original measurements.
- Support comparison of spectra before and after normalization.
- Communicate assumptions and limitations associated with continuum estimation.

### Design Guidance

Continuum treatment is relevant to many spectral analysis methods, including line identification, equivalent-width measurements and comparative spectral studies.

Different spectra may require different continuum treatments. The application should support appropriate approaches without assuming that a single normalization method is suitable for every observation.

## 3.3.5 Spectral Feature Identification

### Core Requirements

- Support identification of emission and absorption features.
- Allow users to select spectral regions for investigation.
- Support identification of candidate atomic, molecular and ionic features.
- Compare observed features with relevant reference information.
- Support identification of spectral lines and molecular bands.
- Accommodate features that may be blended, broadened, shifted or partially obscured.
- Allow users to inspect candidate identifications and their supporting information.
- Distinguish between automatically suggested features and user-confirmed identifications.
- Support multiple possible identifications where the evidence is ambiguous.
- Preserve the spectral context associated with identified features.
- Allow relevant physical and observational conditions to be considered when evaluating candidate identifications.
- Support investigation of how temperature, pressure, chemical abundance, atmospheric conditions and line broadening may influence observed features.
- Account for relevant Doppler shifts, instrumental effects and spectral resolution when assessing possible matches.

### Design Guidance

Spectral feature identification should combine observed measurements with relevant scientific reference information.

An observed feature should not automatically be treated as a confirmed identification simply because a reference transition occurs at a similar position.

The application should account for factors such as spectral resolution, uncertainty, redshift, instrumental effects, overlapping features, physical conditions and the possibility of alternative explanations.

A candidate identification should be evaluated in the context of the complete observation and relevant scientific conditions rather than relying exclusively on the apparent position of an individual feature.

## 3.3.6 Spectral Line and Band Characterization

### Core Requirements

Support characterization of relevant spectral features through measurements and analysis such as:

- Feature position.
- Peak or central wavelength.
- Frequency or wavenumber.
- Feature depth and amplitude.
- Integrated signal.
- Equivalent width.
- Line width and profile characteristics.
- Full width at half maximum (FWHM).
- Line asymmetry and profile structure.
- Band boundaries and integrated band measurements.
- Relative feature strengths.
- Other scientifically appropriate spectral measurements.

Allow users to select appropriate measurement regions and review the methods used.

Preserve relevant measurement units, uncertainties and assumptions associated with calculated properties.

### Design Guidance

Different spectral features require different characterization approaches.

An isolated emission line, an absorption feature and a broad molecular band may require different measurement techniques.

The application should provide appropriate methods for different feature types rather than forcing all spectral measurements into a single analytical model.

## 3.3.7 Quantitative Spectral Analysis and Fitting

### Core Requirements

- Support quantitative analysis of spectral measurements.
- Provide appropriate spectral fitting capabilities.
- Support fitting of individual features and broader spectral regions.
- Accommodate relevant line-profile and continuum models.
- Allow users to select and configure appropriate fitting methods.
- Support estimation of relevant spectral parameters.
- Provide access to fitting results and associated uncertainties where available.
- Allow comparison between observed measurements and fitted models.
- Communicate fitting limitations and unsuccessful or ambiguous results.
- Preserve the original spectrum and distinguish fitted outputs from observed data.
- Support examination of residuals and potential systematic differences between observations and fitted models.

### Design Guidance

Quantitative fitting should be available where the scientific assumptions of the selected method are appropriate.

Users should be able to understand which model was applied, which parameters were estimated and how well the model represents the observations.

The application should avoid presenting a successful numerical fit as automatic confirmation that the underlying scientific interpretation is correct.

## 3.3.8 Molecular, Atomic and Ionic Spectral Analysis

### Core Requirements

- Support investigation of atomic, molecular and ionic spectral features.
- Allow comparison of observed spectra with atomic, molecular and ionic reference information.
- Accommodate different types of spectral transitions and features.
- Support examination of relevant transition positions and associated reference properties.
- Allow users to investigate candidate chemical species.
- Support comparison of multiple candidate species within the same spectral region.
- Preserve information about reference sources and the conditions associated with reference data.
- Distinguish between possible feature matches and scientifically supported identifications.
- Support analysis relevant to planetary atmospheres, exoplanet atmospheres, stellar spectra, solar observations and other astronomical environments.
- Accommodate relevant physical conditions affecting spectral transitions, including temperature, pressure, abundance and line broadening where appropriate.
- Allow investigation of overlapping transitions and blended spectral features.

### Design Guidance

Atomic, molecular and ionic spectroscopy provides important evidence about the composition and physical conditions of astronomical environments.

Reference data may originate from different databases and theoretical or experimental sources. These sources may differ in completeness, accuracy, conditions and intended applications.

The application should preserve the identity and context of reference information and avoid treating every spectral match as equally reliable.

The presence of a possible spectral feature should be distinguished from a scientifically supported identification of a particular chemical species.

## 3.3.9 Spectral Reference Comparison

### Core Requirements

- Allow observed spectra to be compared with reference spectra and spectral information.
- Support comparison with laboratory measurements, astronomical reference observations and theoretical predictions.
- Allow multiple reference spectra or candidate sources to be examined together.
- Support appropriate spectral-axis alignment and unit conversion.
- Accommodate differences in spectral resolution and sampling.
- Allow users to compare spectral features and broader spectral shapes.
- Preserve distinctions between quantitative comparisons and qualitative shape comparisons.
- Display relevant reference information and source attribution.
- Identify limitations that affect the validity of comparisons.
- Support comparison of observed spectra with synthetic or model-generated spectra.
- Allow comparison of complementary observations across different wavelength regions where scientifically appropriate.

### Design Guidance

Reference comparison should support both exploratory investigation and quantitative scientific analysis.

A qualitative resemblance between two spectra may be useful for identifying possible relationships, but it should not be presented as equivalent to a validated quantitative match.

Where numerical comparison is performed, the application should consider relevant differences in units, calibration, resolution, sampling, uncertainty and observational conditions.

Combining observations from different wavelength regions or instruments should be supported where scientifically meaningful, but the application must identify incompatibilities and limitations that could affect the resulting interpretation.

## 3.3.10 Spectral Resolution and Instrumental Effects

### Core Requirements

- Support examination of spectral resolution and sampling.
- Preserve available information about instrumental resolution.
- Allow users to investigate instrumental broadening and related effects.
- Support appropriate resolution matching when comparing spectra.
- Accommodate different instrument configurations and observational conditions.
- Identify when instrumental limitations prevent reliable feature separation.
- Distinguish observed spectral characteristics from effects introduced by instrumentation.
- Communicate when resolution differences may affect analytical conclusions.
- Support examination of signal-to-noise information where available.
- Identify when low signal quality, incomplete spectral coverage or instrumental limitations may prevent reliable feature identification or quantitative analysis.

### Design Guidance

Spectral features can appear different depending on the instrument and observational setup.

Two observations of the same physical feature may differ because of spectral resolution, instrumental response, sampling, signal quality or calibration.

The application should account for these effects where relevant rather than assuming that spectra can be compared directly without considering their observational characteristics.

Signal-to-noise information should be treated as part of the evidence available for evaluating spectral measurements, not as a substitute for broader scientific quality assessment.

## 3.3.11 Multi-Spectrum and Comparative Spectroscopy

### Core Requirements

- Support comparison of multiple spectra from the same astronomical object or system.
- Allow examination of spectra collected at different times or under different observational conditions.
- Support comparison across different instruments and facilities.
- Allow users to compare spectra from different astronomical objects.
- Support examination of changes in spectral features over time.
- Accommodate comparative stellar, planetary and exoplanet research.
- Support comparison of observations with different spectral coverage and resolution.
- Preserve the individual identity, provenance and metadata of each spectrum.
- Allow comparisons to incorporate appropriate normalization, calibration and alignment procedures.
- Support examination of complementary observations across different wavelength regions.
- Allow users to identify gaps in available spectral coverage when comparing observations.
- Support cumulative investigation of repeated observations without treating individual datasets as interchangeable.

### Design Guidance

Comparative spectroscopy should support investigations involving repeated observations, different instruments, multiple objects and broader astronomical populations.

The application should preserve the differences between source observations and make relevant limitations clear when comparing measurements collected under different conditions.

Repeated observations may contribute to a developing research history for an individual object. The application should support examining changes over time while retaining the identity, provenance and observational context of each contributing spectrum.

## 3.3.12 Atmospheric and Exoplanet Spectroscopy

### Core Requirements

Support specialized spectral analysis relevant to planetary and exoplanetary atmospheres, including:

- Transmission spectra.
- Emission spectra.
- Reflection spectra.
- Secondary-eclipse observations.
- Phase-dependent spectral observations.
- Atmospheric spectral feature examination.
- Candidate molecular, atomic and ionic feature investigation.
- Comparison with theoretical atmospheric spectra.
- Examination of wavelength-dependent transit and eclipse measurements.
- Analysis of atmospheric temperature and composition constraints where appropriate.
- Comparison of observations across multiple epochs or instruments.
- Support for evaluating alternative atmospheric interpretations.
- Investigation of atmospheric chemical composition and possible molecular abundances.
- Examination of spectral evidence associated with atmospheric temperature and pressure structures.
- Investigation of spectral effects associated with clouds, hazes and other atmospheric processes.
- Support for examining atmospheric variability and changes between observations where suitable data are available.
- Investigation of how atmospheric properties influence transmission, emission and reflection spectra.

### Design Guidance

Atmospheric spectroscopy often involves measurements that represent indirect constraints on atmospheric properties rather than direct measurements of individual chemical abundances.

The application should support investigation of how observed spectral features relate to atmospheric composition, physical conditions and possible chemical processes.

A potential molecular feature should not automatically be interpreted as a confirmed atmospheric detection. Alternative species, overlapping features, observational limitations and model assumptions must remain part of the investigation.

Atmospheric retrieval, radiative-transfer calculations, forward modelling and inverse modelling are specialized modelling capabilities. Their requirements are established in Section 3.7 and should integrate with the spectral analysis capabilities defined here.

## 3.3.13 Stellar, Solar and Planetary Spectroscopy

### Core Requirements

Support spectral investigations relevant to stellar, solar and planetary research, including:

- Stellar classification and spectral characteristics.
- Stellar atmospheric and chemical composition studies.
- Temperature-sensitive spectral features.
- Radial-velocity-related spectral measurements.
- Stellar activity and variability investigations.
- Solar spectral observations and relevant solar features.
- Planetary atmospheric and surface-related spectral studies.
- Natural satellite and moon-related spectral investigations where appropriate.
- Comparative spectral analysis across different stellar and planetary objects.
- Examination of relevant atomic, molecular and ionic features.
- Comparison with appropriate reference observations and theoretical models.
- Investigation of spectral changes associated with different observational epochs or physical conditions.

### Design Guidance

Spectroscopy should support the different scientific requirements of stellar, solar and planetary research without assuming that atmospheric, surface and stellar spectra can be interpreted using identical methods.

Specialized analytical approaches should remain appropriate to the physical environment and observational technique being investigated.

The application should support spectroscopy as a cross-disciplinary scientific capability rather than restricting it to exoplanetary atmospheric characterization.

## 3.3.14 Spectral Uncertainty and Measurement Limitations

### Core Requirements

- Support the display and examination of spectral measurement uncertainties where available.
- Accommodate uncertainty information associated with individual measurements or derived parameters.
- Preserve source-provided uncertainty information.
- Support appropriate uncertainty propagation during analytical operations.
- Identify missing uncertainty information.
- Distinguish measured uncertainty from estimated or model-derived uncertainty.
- Communicate limitations associated with spectral coverage, signal quality, resolution and sampling.
- Avoid presenting results with unjustified numerical precision.
- Preserve relevant uncertainty information when spectral results are transferred to other capabilities.
- Identify when insufficient signal quality or incomplete observational coverage prevents a reliable interpretation.
- Communicate when an analytical result depends strongly on assumptions or poorly constrained physical conditions.

### Design Guidance

Spectral measurements and derived properties should be interpreted in the context of their uncertainties and observational limitations.

A spectral feature may be visible without being sufficiently constrained for a reliable quantitative measurement.

The application should make these distinctions understandable and avoid implying that the presence of a numerical result establishes its scientific reliability.

Detailed uncertainty handling, validation and scientific safeguards are addressed in Section 5.

## 3.3.15 Spectral Outputs and Integration with Other Capabilities

### Core Requirements

- Allow spectral measurements and analytical results to be used in other scientific capabilities.
- Support export of processed spectra and relevant analytical outputs.
- Preserve metadata, units, provenance and processing information in exported results where supported.
- Allow spectral measurements to contribute to modelling and comparative analysis.
- Support integration with photometric, imaging, atmospheric and other observational measurements where appropriate.
- Allow users to retain and revisit intermediate spectral analysis results.
- Distinguish original observations, processed spectra, fitted models and derived measurements.
- Preserve relationships between spectral results and the investigations that produced them.
- Support integration with atmospheric modelling and retrieval workflows without treating model-derived atmospheric properties as direct spectral measurements.
- Allow spectral results to contribute to broader investigations involving multiple observations and scientific disciplines.

### Design Guidance

Spectral analysis should contribute to broader scientific investigations rather than functioning as an isolated endpoint.

For example, spectral measurements may be combined with photometric observations, atmospheric models or stellar properties to constrain the characteristics of an astronomical object.

The application should preserve the context of these outputs so that they remain understandable and useful when incorporated into subsequent analyses.

## General Principles

Spectroscopy and spectral analysis should:

- Support diverse spectral representations and measurement types.
- Accommodate different wavelength regions and observational techniques.
- Provide clear visualization and interactive examination.
- Support appropriate calibration, correction and normalization procedures.
- Allow identification and characterization of spectral features.
- Provide quantitative analysis and fitting where scientifically appropriate.
- Support atomic, molecular, ionic, stellar, solar, planetary and atmospheric spectroscopy.
- Allow comparison with laboratory, astronomical and theoretical reference information.
- Account for instrumental effects and differences in spectral resolution.
- Support multi-spectrum and interdisciplinary investigations.
- Preserve uncertainty, provenance, metadata and original measurements.
- Distinguish observations, derived measurements, model outputs and scientific interpretations.
- Support both exploratory and advanced analytical workflows.
- Integrate spectral analysis with relevant modelling, photometric and other scientific capabilities.

## General Principle

Spectral analysis should provide users with the tools to investigate the physical and chemical information contained in astronomical spectra while preserving the distinction between observed evidence, analytical measurements and scientific interpretation.
