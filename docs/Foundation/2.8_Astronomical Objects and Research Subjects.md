

# 2.8 — Astronomical Objects and Research Subjects

## Purpose

The application should accommodate a broad range of astronomical objects, systems and phenomena as subjects of scientific investigation.

Scientific disciplines describe areas of research, while astronomical objects and phenomena identify what is being studied. A single object may therefore be relevant to several disciplines, research questions and observational approaches.

The application should support both individual-object investigations and research involving groups, populations, systems and larger astronomical structures.

Its object and subject scope should remain flexible enough to accommodate additional discoveries, classifications and scientific developments without requiring a fixed or exhaustive catalog.

## 2.8.1 Overall Object and Research Subject Scope

### Core Requirements

- Accommodate a broad range of astronomical objects, systems and phenomena.
- Support investigations involving individual objects and groups of related objects.
- Recognize relationships between objects, their parent systems and surrounding environments.
- Allow objects to be studied across multiple scientific disciplines.
- Accommodate different levels of classification and scientific description.
- Support objects whose classifications or properties may be uncertain, disputed or subject to revision.
- Avoid restricting the application to a permanently fixed list of astronomical objects.
- Preserve the scientific identity and context of each research subject.

### Design Guidance

Astronomical objects should not be organized exclusively according to the scientific discipline in which they are studied.

For example, a planet may be relevant to planetary science, observational astronomy, atmospheric chemistry and comparative exoplanet research.

The application should preserve the identity of the object while allowing it to participate in multiple investigations and scientific contexts.

## 2.8.2 Stars and Stellar Objects

### Core Requirements

Include stars and related stellar objects as important research subjects.

Relevant objects include:

- Main-sequence stars.
- Giant and supergiant stars.
- Red dwarfs.
- White dwarfs.
- Brown dwarfs and other substellar objects.
- Variable stars.
- Eruptive and active stars.
- Protostars and young stellar objects.
- Evolved stellar objects.
- Neutron stars.
- Pulsars.
- Black holes and other compact-object research subjects.

Support investigations involving individual stars, stellar populations and relationships between stars and other astronomical objects.

### Design Guidance

Stellar objects may be classified according to different physical, observational and evolutionary characteristics.

The application should accommodate these distinctions without assuming that every object fits into one simple classification system.

Objects near classification boundaries should retain appropriate scientific context and uncertainty.

## 2.8.3 Planets and Planetary Bodies

### Core Requirements

Include planets and other planetary bodies as major research subjects.

Relevant objects include:

- Solar System planets.
- Exoplanets.
- Dwarf planets.
- Natural satellites and moons.
- Rogue or free-floating planetary-mass objects.
- Protoplanets and forming planetary bodies.
- Other scientifically recognized planetary objects.

Support investigations involving planetary physical properties, atmospheres, composition, surfaces, interiors, environments and evolutionary histories.

Recognize that planetary objects may exist within established systems or in environments where their system relationships are uncertain.

### Design Guidance

Planetary objects should retain their individual identities while preserving relationships with their host stars, parent planets, orbital systems and surrounding environments.

The application should accommodate differences between Solar System planets, exoplanets and other planetary-mass objects without assuming they share identical research requirements.

## 2.8.4 Small Solar System Bodies and Related Objects

### Core Requirements

Accommodate smaller objects and components of the Solar System.

Relevant subjects include:

- Asteroids.
- Comets.
- Meteoroids.
- Meteorites.
- Trans-Neptunian objects.
- Kuiper Belt objects.
- Centaurs.
- Other small bodies.
- Interplanetary dust.
- Planetary rings and ring particles.

Support research into their physical properties, composition, orbital characteristics, origins and relationships with larger Solar System bodies.

### Design Guidance

Small Solar System bodies may provide important evidence about planetary formation, Solar System evolution and the distribution of material throughout planetary systems.

Their inclusion should remain possible within the broader scientific scope even if dedicated functionality is not an initial development priority.

## 2.8.5 Planetary Systems and Stellar Systems

### Core Requirements

Support research involving connected astronomical systems rather than only their individual components.

Relevant subjects include:

- Planetary systems.
- Multi-planet systems.
- Star–planet systems.
- Binary star systems.
- Multiple star systems.
- Circumstellar environments.
- Protoplanetary disks.
- Debris disks.
- Planetary rings.
- Other gravitationally or physically connected systems.

Preserve relationships between system components and allow investigations to examine both individual objects and their wider systems.

### Design Guidance

An astronomical system may contain several objects that are scientifically relevant to one another.

For example, an exoplanet investigation may involve its host star, other planets in the system, orbital relationships and circumstellar material.

System relationships should remain available regardless of how individual research materials are organized.

## 2.8.6 Interstellar and Circumstellar Objects and Environments

### Core Requirements

Include astronomical environments and material associated with stars and planetary systems.

Relevant subjects include:

- Interstellar gas and dust.
- Molecular clouds.
- Star-forming regions.
- Circumstellar gas and dust.
- Protoplanetary disks.
- Debris disks.
- Stellar winds and outflows.
- Nebulae.
- Planetary nebulae.
- Supernova remnants.
- Other structures associated with stellar formation and evolution.

Support research into their physical properties, chemical composition, structure, dynamics and relationships with surrounding astronomical objects.

### Design Guidance

Astronomical environments may be both research subjects in their own right and important contexts for investigating other objects.

For example, a molecular cloud may be studied through astrochemistry, star formation, spectroscopy and interstellar medium research.

The application should accommodate these overlapping scientific relationships.

## 2.8.7 Galaxies and Large-Scale Astronomical Structures

### Core Requirements

Allow research involving galaxies and larger astronomical structures within the application's broader scientific scope.

Relevant subjects include:

- Spiral galaxies.
- Elliptical galaxies.
- Irregular galaxies.
- Dwarf galaxies.
- Active galaxies.
- Galaxy groups.
- Galaxy clusters.
- Superclusters.
- Filaments and other large-scale structures.
- Intergalactic environments.

Support scientific investigations involving their physical properties, composition, formation, evolution, interactions and large-scale distribution.

### Design Guidance

Galactic and large-scale research may require different observational scales, datasets and scientific methods from individual-object investigations.

Their inclusion should allow future expansion without requiring these areas to receive the same initial development priority as planetary, stellar and exoplanet research.

## 2.8.8 Compact Objects and High-Energy Phenomena

### Core Requirements

Accommodate compact astronomical objects and phenomena associated with extreme physical conditions.

Relevant subjects include:

- Black holes.
- Neutron stars.
- Pulsars.
- Magnetars.
- Accretion disks.
- Relativistic jets.
- Supernovae.
- Gamma-ray bursts.
- Other high-energy and transient astronomical phenomena.

Support investigations involving their physical characteristics, radiation, interactions, formation and evolution.

### Design Guidance

These subjects connect stellar evolution with high-energy astrophysics, relativistic physics and observational astronomy.

Their inclusion should preserve opportunities for future scientific expansion without implying that specialized high-energy analysis capabilities must be part of the initial implementation.

## 2.8.9 Transient and Time-Dependent Astronomical Phenomena

### Core Requirements

Recognize astronomical phenomena that change significantly over time or occur as temporary events.

Relevant subjects include:

- Supernova explosions.
- Novae.
- Stellar flares.
- Variable stellar phenomena.
- Transient high-energy events.
- Tidal disruption events.
- Gamma-ray bursts.
- Cometary activity.
- Occultations and eclipses.
- Other short-lived or evolving astronomical events.

Support investigations involving event timing, repeated observations, temporal development and relationships with the objects responsible for the phenomena.

### Design Guidance

Transient phenomena may be associated with individual objects, interacting systems or broader astrophysical processes.

The application should preserve the distinction between the astronomical object, the event being investigated and the observations used to study it.

## 2.8.10 Classification, Identity and Scientific Relationships

### Core Requirements

- Accommodate established astronomical classification systems.
- Allow objects to have multiple relevant classifications where scientifically appropriate.
- Preserve object identity independently of individual investigations or datasets.
- Support relationships between objects, systems, environments and associated phenomena.
- Accommodate uncertain, provisional or disputed classifications.
- Recognize that scientific classifications may change as new evidence becomes available.
- Avoid treating organizational categories as permanent scientific definitions.
- Preserve relationships when an object's classification or scientific understanding changes.

### Design Guidance

An astronomical object may have several relevant scientific descriptions depending on its properties, context and research purpose.

For example, an object may be classified by its physical characteristics, evolutionary state, observational behaviour or relationship with another system.

The application should distinguish scientific classification from user-defined organization. Organizing an object into a particular project category must not alter its scientific identity.

## 2.8.11 Expanding the Object and Research Subject Catalog

### Core Requirements

- Allow additional astronomical objects and phenomena to be accommodated as scientific knowledge develops.
- Avoid requiring every object type to be defined in advance.
- Support newly discovered objects and newly recognized categories.
- Accommodate revised classifications and updated scientific understanding.
- Allow related objects to be grouped according to scientifically meaningful relationships.
- Support research involving objects that do not fit neatly into existing categories.

### Design Guidance

The application should not attempt to establish a permanently exhaustive catalog of astronomical objects.

Scientific discoveries, revised classifications and emerging research areas will continually expand the range of subjects astronomers investigate.

The object framework should remain adaptable while maintaining consistent scientific identity, classification and relationships.

## General Principles

The application's astronomical object and research subject framework should:

- Accommodate a broad range of astronomical objects, systems and phenomena.
- Support both individual-object and system-level investigations.
- Distinguish astronomical objects from scientific disciplines and research methods.
- Preserve relationships between objects and their wider environments.
- Accommodate multiple scientific classifications where appropriate.
- Recognize uncertain, provisional and evolving scientific classifications.
- Avoid restricting research to an exhaustive predefined object catalog.
- Allow objects to participate in multiple scientific disciplines and investigations.
- Support future discoveries and expansion into additional astronomical subjects.

## General Principle

Astronomical objects and phenomena should form a flexible foundation for scientific investigation, allowing users to study individual subjects, connected systems and broader astronomical structures across multiple disciplines.

## Implementation Boundary

This subsection establishes the types of astronomical objects, systems and phenomena that may fall within the application's scientific scope.

It does not require every listed object category to receive dedicated functionality or equal development priority.

The application should establish its initial object support according to the scientific priorities and implementation requirements defined elsewhere in the Foundations documentation.

Specific object management, data relationships, research workflows, classification tools and visualization capabilities are addressed in the relevant application structure and scientific capability sections.
