# 9.8 — Scalability, Maintenance and Long-Term Sustainability

## Purpose

Establishes the requirements for ensuring the application can grow, remain maintainable and continue operating reliably over an extended period.

The application is intended to become a substantial scientific platform with multiple disciplines, workspaces, computational capabilities, external integrations, research projects and potentially collaborative infrastructure.

Its architecture, development practices and operational systems must accommodate this growth without making the application unnecessarily difficult to maintain, extend or operate.

Long-term sustainability includes more than technical scalability. It also involves dependency management, security, documentation, testing, infrastructure costs, resource requirements, recovery, compatibility and the ability to maintain scientific reliability as the application evolves.

The objective is to establish a sustainable development and maintenance foundation that supports ambitious long-term expansion without requiring unnecessary infrastructure or complexity during initial development.

## 9.8.1 — Scalability Principles

### Functional Requirements

- Design the application to accommodate growth in scientific capabilities and supported disciplines.
- Support increasing numbers of users, projects, datasets and research materials.
- Accommodate increasingly complex scientific workflows and computational requirements.
- Allow application components to expand without unnecessarily restructuring unrelated functionality.
- Identify potential scalability limitations as the application develops.
- Evaluate scalability according to actual requirements rather than speculative future demand.
- Preserve application responsiveness and reliability as functionality increases.
- Avoid unnecessary architectural restrictions that prevent future expansion.
- Maintain manageable development and operational complexity.
- Support incremental improvements to the application's infrastructure.

### Design Guidance

Scalability should be considered from the beginning, but should not become an excuse for implementing infrastructure that is not yet needed.

The application should begin with a practical architecture that can be expanded when actual requirements justify it.

The objective is to avoid both premature optimization and architectural decisions that make future expansion unnecessarily difficult.

## 9.8.2 — Application and User Scalability

### Functional Requirements

- Accommodate increasing numbers of users where applicable.
- Support growth in the number and complexity of research projects.
- Maintain efficient access to scientific data and application functionality.
- Evaluate the effects of concurrent users and operations.
- Support appropriate management of user sessions and application resources.
- Consider increased database and storage requirements.
- Identify performance limitations as usage grows.
- Maintain consistent application behaviour across supported environments.
- Support expansion of collaborative functionality where introduced.
- Preserve user privacy and permissions as the application scales.

### Design Guidance

The application should not assume that its initial usage patterns will represent its long-term requirements.

Growth may involve more users, larger projects, additional scientific disciplines, more external integrations and increasingly demanding research activities.

Infrastructure should be expanded according to demonstrated needs rather than introducing large-scale hosting and management systems prematurely.

## 9.8.3 — Scientific Data and Storage Scalability

### Functional Requirements

- Accommodate growth in the volume and variety of scientific data.
- Support increasing numbers of observations, spectra, photometric datasets, images and research records.
- Maintain efficient data retrieval and storage.
- Avoid unnecessary duplication of large datasets.
- Preserve data integrity as storage requirements increase.
- Support expansion of local and remote storage where appropriate.
- Identify storage capacity limitations.
- Allow storage infrastructure to be expanded or migrated.
- Maintain backup, recovery and archival capabilities.
- Preserve project relationships and provenance during storage expansion or migration.

### Design Guidance

Astronomical research may involve substantial datasets, particularly when working with large archives, multiwavelength observations, time-series measurements and computational outputs.

Storage architecture should accommodate this growth without requiring all data to be stored in one location or through one provider.

Large-data management should remain proportionate to actual scientific requirements.

## 9.8.4 — Computational Scalability

### Functional Requirements

- Accommodate increasing computational demands as scientific capabilities expand.
- Support increasingly complex numerical methods, models and simulations.
- Allow additional computational resources to be introduced where necessary.
- Evaluate local and remote processing requirements.
- Support appropriate parallel processing and hardware acceleration.
- Identify computational bottlenecks.
- Preserve scientific accuracy and numerical reliability during performance improvements.
- Allow computational infrastructure to evolve independently where appropriate.
- Maintain reproducibility across computational environments.
- Avoid requiring advanced distributed computing before it is justified.

### Design Guidance

Computational requirements may grow significantly as the application introduces more sophisticated atmospheric models, retrieval methods, simulations and large-scale parameter exploration.

The application should be capable of adopting more advanced computational resources when necessary.

However, computational complexity should grow in response to real workloads rather than anticipated maximum requirements.

## 9.8.5 — Performance Monitoring and Optimization

### Functional Requirements

- Monitor important application performance characteristics.
- Identify slow operations and resource bottlenecks.
- Evaluate performance across scientific workspaces and shared functionality.
- Consider memory, processor, storage and network utilization.
- Identify performance problems affecting large datasets and computational workloads.
- Support systematic testing and benchmarking.
- Evaluate optimization improvements against actual performance measurements.
- Preserve scientific accuracy during performance optimization.
- Identify regressions introduced by application changes.
- Communicate significant performance limitations where appropriate.

### Design Guidance

Performance optimization should be evidence-driven.

The application should not introduce complex caching, parallelization or infrastructure changes simply because they may theoretically improve performance.

Instead, identify actual bottlenecks, determine their causes and implement proportionate improvements.

Performance testing should include representative scientific workloads rather than relying exclusively on simple application demonstrations.

## 9.8.6 — Software Maintenance and Dependency Lifecycle

### Functional Requirements

- Maintain an organized inventory of important software dependencies.
- Track relevant package versions and compatibility requirements.
- Monitor dependency maintenance and security status.
- Identify outdated, deprecated or unsupported technologies.
- Evaluate dependency updates before adopting them.
- Test compatibility after significant updates.
- Document important dependency changes.
- Identify alternatives when essential dependencies become unavailable.
- Avoid unnecessary dependence on abandoned or poorly maintained packages.
- Maintain reproducible development and testing environments.
- Preserve compatibility information for historical scientific analyses where relevant.

### Design Guidance

The application should prefer established and actively maintained technologies where appropriate.

However, dependency popularity alone should not determine suitability. Scientific reliability, maintenance quality, licensing, security and long-term compatibility must also be considered.

Dependency updates should be managed carefully to avoid unexpectedly changing scientific functionality or invalidating historical reproducibility.

## 9.8.7 — Application Updates and Version Management

### Functional Requirements

- Support controlled application updates.
- Maintain clear application version information.
- Document significant changes between versions.
- Preserve user projects and research data during updates.
- Evaluate database and file-format migrations before applying them.
- Identify potentially breaking changes.
- Support appropriate rollback or recovery mechanisms where practical.
- Avoid silently changing historical scientific results.
- Communicate changes affecting scientific methods, dependencies or external integrations.
- Maintain compatibility information for supported application versions.
- Allow users to understand which application version was used for previous analyses.

### Design Guidance

Application updates should not compromise existing research.

Changes to scientific algorithms, processing methods or data structures may affect analytical results and reproducibility.

Such changes should be documented and handled through appropriate versioning and migration procedures.

Where necessary, previous methods and analysis environments should remain identifiable and accessible.

## 9.8.8 — Security Maintenance and Vulnerability Management

### Functional Requirements

- Maintain awareness of relevant application and dependency vulnerabilities.
- Evaluate security updates and apply appropriate fixes.
- Protect user accounts, credentials, research materials and external integrations.
- Review security implications of significant architectural changes.
- Identify outdated or unsupported security dependencies.
- Maintain appropriate access controls and authentication mechanisms.
- Protect stored and transmitted information.
- Document significant security problems and their resolution.
- Communicate security issues when they materially affect users.
- Preserve relevant security records without unnecessarily exposing sensitive information.

### Design Guidance

Security should be treated as an ongoing maintenance responsibility rather than a one-time implementation task.

The application should prioritize established security practices and regularly evaluate the security of its dependencies, infrastructure and integrations.

Security improvements should not unnecessarily compromise scientific data portability or user control.

## 9.8.9 — Reliability, Monitoring and Operational Maintenance

### Functional Requirements

- Monitor important application services and infrastructure.
- Identify application failures and service interruptions.
- Maintain relevant error and operational records.
- Detect unavailable external dependencies.
- Support appropriate health checks and diagnostics.
- Identify recurring failures and performance problems.
- Preserve application and research data during failures.
- Support recovery of important application services.
- Maintain clear records of significant operational incidents.
- Avoid exposing credentials or private research information through diagnostic systems.
- Support monitoring appropriate to both local and hosted environments.

### Design Guidance

Operational monitoring should provide enough information to identify and investigate problems without creating excessive logging or maintenance overhead.

The application should distinguish between failures in its own functionality and failures caused by external services.

Local application functionality should remain available where possible when unrelated hosted services are unavailable.

## 9.8.10 — Backup, Disaster Recovery and Business Continuity

### Functional Requirements

- Maintain reliable backup and recovery procedures.
- Protect important application data, research projects, configurations and documentation.
- Consider failures involving local hardware, storage systems, databases and hosted infrastructure.
- Support restoration following significant failures.
- Verify that backups can be restored successfully.
- Document recovery procedures.
- Identify potential data-loss risks.
- Maintain appropriate backup retention.
- Preserve important research history and provenance during recovery.
- Evaluate recovery procedures periodically.
- Communicate recovery limitations where complete restoration is not possible.

### Design Guidance

Recovery planning should consider both ordinary application interruptions and more serious failures involving hardware, databases or infrastructure.

Backup systems should be designed around the importance of the information being protected.

The application should not assume that a backup is reliable without appropriate restoration testing.

## 9.8.11 — Development and Testing Sustainability

### Functional Requirements

- Maintain development practices that support continued improvement.
- Support automated testing and regression testing.
- Maintain appropriate scientific and technical validation tests.
- Allow components to be tested independently where practical.
- Maintain consistent development environments.
- Document build, testing and deployment procedures.
- Identify technical debt and maintenance risks.
- Evaluate the effects of changes on existing functionality.
- Support code review and quality checks where appropriate.
- Maintain understandable and organized source code.
- Preserve test results and known limitations.

### Design Guidance

Development sustainability is particularly important for a project expected to grow substantially over time.

Code should remain understandable to future developers and AI development partners.

Testing should cover both application functionality and scientific correctness where appropriate.

Technical debt should be identified and managed rather than allowed to accumulate invisibly.

## 9.8.12 — Documentation and Architectural Continuity

### Functional Requirements

- Maintain current technical and architectural documentation.
- Document major components, dependencies and integration points.
- Maintain development records and significant architectural decisions.
- Track implemented, incomplete and planned capabilities.
- Record known limitations, unresolved problems and maintenance concerns.
- Update documentation when functionality or architecture changes.
- Maintain a reliable record of project status and future work.
- Support continuity between development sessions and different AI development partners.
- Identify outdated documentation and inconsistencies with implementation.
- Preserve historical decisions and their reasoning.

### Design Guidance

Documentation should function as the application's long-term institutional memory.

Development should not depend on previous conversations or the memory of individual developers.

The documentation and continuity requirements established in Section 11 should be treated as essential development infrastructure.

## 9.8.13 — Cost and Operational Sustainability

### Functional Requirements

- Evaluate development and operational costs.
- Consider hosting, storage, computational resources and external service expenses.
- Identify ongoing subscription and licensing costs.
- Consider maintenance and infrastructure management requirements.
- Evaluate cost implications of scaling users, datasets and computational workloads.
- Avoid unnecessary paid dependencies and infrastructure.
- Identify cost-effective alternatives where appropriate.
- Communicate significant recurring costs and resource requirements.
- Consider the long-term financial sustainability of external integrations.
- Preserve practical access to core scientific functionality without unnecessary recurring costs.

### Design Guidance

The application should not assume that increasing infrastructure expenditure is the default solution to every technical problem.

Cost should be evaluated alongside scientific value, reliability, performance and maintenance effort.

The architecture should remain financially practical to develop and operate, particularly during early stages.

## 9.8.14 — Long-Term Scientific and Technical Sustainability

### Functional Requirements

- Preserve the ability to maintain and extend the application over extended periods.
- Avoid unnecessary dependence on discontinued services or technologies.
- Maintain access to important scientific methods and historical analysis records.
- Reassess technologies and dependencies as the scientific and software landscape changes.
- Allow additional scientific disciplines and research capabilities to be introduced.
- Preserve interoperability and portability as the architecture evolves.
- Identify obsolete functionality and technical limitations.
- Support gradual modernization without unnecessarily disrupting existing research.
- Maintain the distinction between historical results and newer scientific methods.
- Consider the long-term accessibility of research projects and their dependencies.

### Design Guidance

The application should be designed as a long-term scientific platform rather than a short-lived software project.

Its architecture and development practices should accommodate changes in scientific methods, data standards, software ecosystems and computational technologies.

Modernization should preserve historical research wherever possible rather than silently replacing earlier methods and results.

## Future Possibilities

The following capabilities may be investigated as the application develops but are not initial implementation priorities.

- Advanced infrastructure monitoring.
- Automated scaling of hosted services.
- Distributed deployment environments.
- Advanced performance profiling and analytics.
- Automated dependency and vulnerability assessment.
- More extensive disaster recovery infrastructure.
- Advanced application update and rollback systems.
- Institutional and large-scale deployments.
- Automated technical debt identification.
- Long-term scientific software preservation strategies.
- Advanced infrastructure cost monitoring.
- More extensive automated compatibility testing.

These capabilities should be introduced according to actual application scale, operational requirements and available resources.

## Initial Implementation Priorities

The initial development and infrastructure strategy should prioritize:

- A maintainable and understandable architecture.
- Clear component responsibilities and dependencies.
- Sensible dependency and version management.
- Basic application performance monitoring.
- Automated testing and regression protection.
- Scientific validation of important methods.
- Reliable backup and recovery foundations.
- Controlled application updates.
- Basic security and vulnerability management.
- Current technical and architectural documentation.
- Awareness of infrastructure and maintenance costs.
- A clear record of technical limitations and outstanding problems.
- Compatibility with future expansion and hybrid deployment.

Advanced distributed infrastructure, automated scaling and extensive operational management systems are not initial requirements unless technical research demonstrates a genuine need.

## Implementation Considerations

Scalability, maintenance and sustainability should be informed by research into:

- Application architecture and modularity.
- Performance and scalability testing.
- Database and storage expansion.
- Computational infrastructure.
- Dependency and package maintenance.
- Software versioning and migrations.
- Security maintenance.
- Monitoring and diagnostics.
- Backup and disaster recovery.
- Automated testing and scientific validation.
- Hosting and operational costs.
- Long-term scientific software sustainability.

The AI development partner should evaluate the application's maintenance requirements throughout development rather than treating them as a final preparation task.

Significant infrastructure and technology decisions should be documented with their reasoning, limitations, costs and alternatives.

The application should grow incrementally, with additional complexity introduced only when actual requirements justify it.

**General Principle:** Build and maintain an application that can grow substantially without becoming unnecessarily difficult or expensive to develop, operate and maintain. Prioritize understandable architecture, reliable testing, security, documentation, controlled updates, data preservation and long-term scientific continuity. Scalability should support the application's ambitions without creating unnecessary complexity before it is needed.
