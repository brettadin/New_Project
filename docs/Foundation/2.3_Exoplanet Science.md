

# 2.3 — Exoplanet Science

## Purpose

Exoplanet science is one of the application's primary scientific disciplines. It encompasses the discovery, study and characterization of planets beyond the Solar System, their host stars, planetary systems, atmospheres and broader physical environments.

The application should support a broad range of exoplanet research, from examining individual planetary observations to investigating planetary populations, atmospheric composition, system evolution and potential habitability.

Exoplanet science should be treated as an integrated research subject, connecting observational astronomy, spectroscopy, planetary science, stellar astronomy, chemistry, atmospheric science and computational methods.

## 2.3.1 Exoplanet Discovery and Detection

### Core Requirements

- Include the discovery and detection of planets outside the Solar System within the application's scientific scope.
- Recognize established exoplanet detection approaches, including:
  - Transit photometry.
  - Radial velocity measurements.
  - Direct imaging.
  - Gravitational microlensing.
  - Astrometry.
  - Transit timing variations and related timing methods.
- Accommodate research involving individual detections, candidate planets and confirmed exoplanets.
- Recognize that different detection methods provide different types of evidence and constrain different planetary properties.
- Preserve distinctions between confirmed planets, candidates, disputed detections and subsequently rejected objects.
- Support research into the limitations, uncertainties and observational biases associated with different detection methods.

### Design Guidance

Exoplanet detection should not be treated as a single observational technique. Different methods provide complementary evidence and may be combined to improve our understanding of a planetary system.

The scientific status of an object should remain clear. A candidate detection must not automatically be presented as equivalent to a confirmed planet.

## 2.3.2 Exoplanet Properties and Characterization

### Core Requirements

Support research into the physical and orbital properties of exoplanets, including:

- Planetary radius and mass.
- Mean density and related bulk physical properties.
- Orbital period and orbital distance.
- Orbital eccentricity and inclination.
- Equilibrium temperature and other relevant temperature estimates.
- Planetary structure and possible internal composition.
- Rotation and other physical characteristics where observations permit.
- Planetary system architecture and relationships between individual planets.
- Relationships between planetary properties and their host stars.

The application should accommodate properties obtained through direct measurement, derived calculations, model-dependent estimates and indirect inference.

### Design Guidance

Scientific properties must retain their measurement context. A reported value should not be treated as equally certain or equally direct as every other value.

Where appropriate, distinguish:

- Directly observed quantities.
- Derived quantities calculated from observations.
- Model-dependent estimates.
- Inferred physical properties.
- Assumed or adopted parameters.

Uncertainty, measurement limitations and relevant assumptions should remain associated with the corresponding information.

## 2.3.3 Exoplanetary Systems and Orbital Characteristics

### Core Requirements

Include research into the structure, dynamics and evolution of planetary systems beyond the Solar System.

Relevant subjects include:

- Single-planet and multi-planet systems.
- Planetary orbital configurations.
- Orbital resonances and gravitational interactions.
- Planetary migration and orbital evolution.
- Relationships between planetary masses, orbital distances and system architecture.
- Formation and evolution of planetary systems.
- Interactions between planets and their host stars.
- Comparisons between exoplanetary systems and the Solar System.

### Design Guidance

Exoplanets should not be considered exclusively as isolated objects. Their properties and evolution are often closely connected to the characteristics and dynamics of their wider planetary systems.

Research involving individual planets should retain relevant system context whenever that information is available.

## 2.3.4 Exoplanetary Atmospheres and Composition

### Core Requirements

Atmospheric characterization is a major component of the application's exoplanet science scope.

Include research into:

- Atmospheric composition and chemical species.
- Molecular and atomic constituents.
- Atmospheric temperature and pressure structure.
- Temperature profiles and possible thermal gradients.
- Atmospheric stratification and vertical structure.
- Clouds, hazes and aerosols.
- Atmospheric circulation and heat redistribution.
- Atmospheric escape and interaction with stellar radiation.
- Photochemistry and atmospheric chemical processes.
- Atmospheric formation, evolution and chemical enrichment.
- Relationships between atmospheric properties and planetary characteristics.

Recognize spectroscopy as an important source of atmospheric information, including research involving transmission, emission and reflection spectra.

Accommodate investigations of both individual atmospheric constituents and broader atmospheric composition.

### Design Guidance

Atmospheric characterization is an inference process rather than a direct reading of a planet's complete atmospheric composition.

Observed spectral features, candidate molecular identifications, retrieved atmospheric properties and model predictions must remain distinguishable.

The presence of a possible spectral feature should not automatically be treated as a confirmed chemical detection.

The application should accommodate competing interpretations and uncertainty in atmospheric characterization.

## 2.3.5 Exoplanet Observations and Observable Phenomena

### Core Requirements

Include the scientific study of observational phenomena used to investigate exoplanets and their atmospheres.

Relevant subjects include:

- Primary transits.
- Secondary eclipses.
- Transmission spectroscopy.
- Emission spectroscopy.
- Reflection spectroscopy.
- Phase curves.
- Thermal emission.
- Reflected stellar radiation.
- Planetary brightness variations.
- Transit timing and duration variations.
- Time-dependent atmospheric and planetary signals.

Recognize that different observational configurations provide different information about a planet and its atmosphere.

### Design Guidance

These observational phenomena should be understood in their scientific context rather than treated as interchangeable forms of data.

For example, transmission and emission spectroscopy probe different atmospheric conditions and viewing geometries. Their interpretations should retain the context of the original observation.

Detailed handling and analysis of these observations belongs in Section 3.

## 2.3.6 Comparative Exoplanet Science and Planetary Populations

### Core Requirements

Support research that examines relationships and patterns across multiple exoplanets and planetary systems.

Relevant research subjects include:

- Comparisons between planets of different sizes, masses and compositions.
- Comparisons between different atmospheric types.
- Planetary population distributions.
- Relationships between planetary properties and orbital characteristics.
- Relationships between planets and host-star properties.
- Classification and categorization of exoplanets.
- Population trends and observational selection effects.
- Comparisons between different planetary system architectures.
- Comparisons between exoplanets and Solar System planets.

### Design Guidance

Comparative research should accommodate both individual-object investigations and broader population-level studies.

Comparisons must account for differences in observational coverage, measurement quality, uncertainty, assumptions and available data. Apparent differences between planets should not automatically be interpreted as physical differences when observational limitations may explain them.

## 2.3.7 Habitability and Astrobiological Research

### Core Requirements

Include scientific investigations into the conditions that may influence the habitability of exoplanets.

Relevant subjects include:

- Habitable-zone concepts and their limitations.
- Planetary temperatures and energy balance.
- Atmospheric composition and structure.
- Potential liquid-water conditions.
- Stellar radiation and activity.
- Atmospheric retention and escape.
- Planetary magnetic environments where relevant.
- Chemical conditions associated with potential habitability.
- Biosignature research and the interpretation of possible biological indicators.

Support research that connects planetary properties, atmospheric conditions and host-star characteristics when investigating potential habitability.

### Design Guidance

Habitability and the detection of life are distinct scientific questions.

The application must distinguish between:

- Conditions that may permit habitability.
- Evidence consistent with potentially habitable environments.
- Chemical or atmospheric features proposed as possible biosignatures.
- Evidence that has been independently evaluated and supported.
- Confirmed evidence of biological activity, where such a conclusion is scientifically justified.

Potential biosignatures must not be presented as proof of life without sufficient evidence.

Research in this area should accommodate alternative explanations, false-positive scenarios, observational limitations and uncertainty.

## 2.3.8 Host Stars and Star–Planet Relationships

### Core Requirements

Recognize host-star properties as an essential part of exoplanet research.

Relevant subjects include:

- Stellar mass, radius and luminosity.
- Effective temperature and spectral classification.
- Stellar composition and metallicity.
- Stellar activity and variability.
- Stellar radiation and its influence on planetary environments.
- Stellar age and evolutionary state.
- Star–planet interactions.
- Relationships between host-star properties and planetary formation, evolution and atmospheric conditions.

### Design Guidance

Host-star research should be integrated with exoplanet investigations without duplicating the broader scientific scope of stellar astronomy defined in Section 2.5.

The application should preserve relationships between planets and their host stars so that relevant stellar information can contribute to planetary characterization and interpretation.

## 2.3.9 Interdisciplinary Relationships

### Core Requirements

Recognize that exoplanet science naturally overlaps with several other scientific disciplines, including:

- Planetary science.
- Stellar astronomy.
- Observational astronomy.
- Spectroscopy and molecular science.
- Chemistry and atmospheric chemistry.
- Atmospheric physics.
- Astrophysics.
- Computational and theoretical science.
- Astrobiology.

Allow exoplanet investigations to draw upon relevant scientific concepts, observations, datasets and methods from these disciplines.

### Design Guidance

Exoplanet research should not be artificially restricted to a single scientific category.

An investigation into an exoplanet atmosphere, for example, may involve spectroscopy, molecular identification, atmospheric modelling, stellar characterization and planetary science.

The application should accommodate these relationships while preserving the scientific context and provenance of the materials involved.

## General Principles

Exoplanet science within the application should:

- Support discovery, characterization and comparative research.
- Accommodate individual planets and complete planetary systems.
- Treat atmospheric characterization as a major research subject.
- Recognize the importance of host stars and stellar environments.
- Accommodate different observational methods and sources of evidence.
- Preserve distinctions between observations, derived quantities, model outputs and scientific interpretations.
- Support population-level research and interdisciplinary investigations.
- Treat habitability and biosignature research with appropriate scientific caution.
- Preserve uncertainty, assumptions and observational limitations.

## General Principle

Exoplanet science should be supported as a connected field of research in which planetary properties, atmospheres, host stars, observations and system evolution contribute to a broader understanding of planets beyond the Solar System.

## Implementation Boundary

This subsection establishes the scientific subjects and research questions that fall within the application's exoplanet science scope.

It does not require every detection technique, observational method, atmospheric model or research capability to be available in the initial implementation.

Specific scientific tools, analytical workflows, modelling capabilities, data handling and visualization requirements are defined elsewhere in the Foundations documentation, particularly Section 3.
