

# 4.8 — Data Quality, Reliability and Availability

## Purpose

The application should help users understand the quality, reliability and availability of scientific data and external resources.

Astronomical data can vary substantially in completeness, calibration, precision, uncertainty, resolution and observational limitations. External archives and databases may also differ in reliability, documentation, maintenance and service availability.

The application should preserve available quality information, identify potential problems and communicate limitations that may affect the interpretation or use of scientific resources.

Data quality should be considered in relation to the intended research activity rather than treated as a single universal measure. A dataset may be useful for exploratory investigation while remaining unsuitable for a particular quantitative analysis.

The application should distinguish information supplied by original providers from assessments generated through its own inspection and evaluation.

## 4.8.1 Dataset Quality Information

### Core Requirements

Preserve and communicate available information about the quality and characteristics of individual datasets.

Relevant characteristics may include:

- Completeness of measurements and observations.
- Missing, invalid or non-numerical values.
- Measurement uncertainty and precision.
- Calibration status and available calibration information.
- Instrumental limitations.
- Spectral, spatial and temporal resolution.
- Sensitivity and detection limits.
- Sampling frequency and observational coverage.
- Signal-to-noise characteristics.
- Background contamination and instrumental artifacts.
- Available quality flags and validation indicators.
- Processing and reduction history.
- Known observational limitations.
- Completeness of associated metadata.

Distinguish between quality information supplied by the original source and assessments generated by the application.

Identify when important quality information is unavailable rather than assuming that the absence of a warning indicates good data quality.

### Design Guidance

Data quality should not be reduced to a single numerical score.

Different characteristics may affect different types of scientific investigation. A dataset with limited resolution may still be useful for examining broad features, while another dataset with extensive coverage may contain significant calibration limitations.

The application should preserve individual quality characteristics so users can evaluate their relevance to the intended research.

## 4.8.2 Original Quality Flags and Validation Information

### Core Requirements

- Preserve quality flags and warnings supplied by original data providers.
- Retain relevant definitions and documentation explaining source-specific quality indicators.
- Distinguish original quality information from application-generated assessments.
- Identify missing, incomplete, inconsistent or potentially invalid information.
- Preserve available calibration and validation indicators.
- Communicate known processing and observational limitations.
- Identify potential artifacts, contamination and other measurement problems where detectable.
- Preserve relevant warnings when data is retrieved, imported, converted or transferred.
- Avoid silently removing or overriding original quality flags.
- Distinguish confirmed problems from potential concerns and unresolved uncertainties.

### Design Guidance

Different archives, instruments and scientific disciplines may use different quality conventions.

The application should not assume that similarly named flags represent identical conditions across unrelated resources.

Where original quality information is presented in a standardized form, users should still be able to inspect the original values and their definitions.

Application-generated warnings should remain distinguishable from warnings supplied by the original provider.

## 4.8.3 Data Completeness and Missing Information

### Core Requirements

- Identify missing or incomplete measurements where detectable.
- Distinguish missing information from measured values, including numerical zero.
- Identify incomplete observations and partial data products.
- Preserve information about gaps in spectral, spatial or temporal coverage.
- Identify missing uncertainty, calibration or observational metadata where relevant.
- Communicate when a dataset contains only a subset of the information available from its original source.
- Preserve available information even when other portions are incomplete.
- Avoid silently filling missing values or presenting incomplete data as complete.
- Allow users to inspect the extent and location of missing information where practical.

### Design Guidance

Missing information can have different causes and scientific implications.

A missing measurement may reflect an observational gap, instrument limitation, unavailable data product or processing decision.

The application should distinguish between these situations where the available information permits.

Incomplete datasets should not automatically be rejected when they remain useful for other research purposes.

## 4.8.4 Calibration, Measurement and Instrumental Limitations

### Core Requirements

Preserve and communicate available information about the calibration and measurement conditions of scientific data.

This may include:

- Calibration status.
- Calibration methods and available reference information.
- Instrument response and sensitivity.
- Wavelength or frequency calibration.
- Photometric calibration.
- Background and detector corrections.
- Instrumental resolution.
- Measurement precision.
- Known systematic effects.
- Observational conditions affecting measurement quality.
- Available uncertainty and error estimates.

Identify when calibration information is missing, incomplete or unknown.

Distinguish calibrated, partially calibrated, uncalibrated and unknown data states where the available information supports these classifications.

Avoid assuming that data is calibrated simply because it was retrieved from an established archive or supplied in a recognized scientific format.

### Design Guidance

Calibration and instrumental limitations can directly affect scientific interpretation.

For example, two spectra may have different instrumental resolutions or calibration histories even when they cover the same wavelength range.

The application should retain these distinctions and make relevant limitations accessible when users compare or analyze datasets.

## 4.8.5 Scientific Suitability and Data Limitations

### Core Requirements

Evaluate dataset suitability in relation to the intended scientific operation where sufficient information is available.

Consider relevant factors such as:

- Whether measurements represent the quantities required by an analysis.
- Whether observational coverage is sufficient.
- Whether resolution and sensitivity are appropriate.
- Whether calibration information is adequate.
- Whether units and measurement conventions are known.
- Whether uncertainty information is available and suitable.
- Whether instrumental and observational limitations are understood.
- Whether relevant metadata is complete enough for the intended operation.
- Whether known data-quality problems may affect the expected results.

Communicate when data is:

- Suitable for the intended operation based on available information.
- Potentially suitable but subject to identifiable limitations.
- Partially suitable for selected operations.
- Unsuitable for the intended operation.
- Of undetermined suitability because essential information is missing.

Explain relevant limitations where possible.

Avoid presenting suitability assessments as universal judgments about the scientific value of a dataset.

### Design Guidance

A dataset's suitability depends on the research question and analytical method.

For example, a low-resolution spectrum may be useful for examining broad spectral characteristics but unsuitable for resolving closely spaced spectral features.

Likewise, incomplete time-series coverage may prevent a particular periodicity analysis while still allowing other forms of investigation.

The application should communicate the specific limitation rather than simply labeling an entire dataset as good or bad.

Detailed scientific compatibility and preparation requirements are addressed in Section 3.2.

## 4.8.6 Inconsistent, Conflicting and Unusual Data

### Core Requirements

Identify potentially significant inconsistencies within datasets and between related scientific resources.

Where appropriate, detect or communicate:

- Conflicting object identifiers.
- Inconsistent units or measurement conventions.
- Unexpected numerical ranges.
- Missing or duplicated observations.
- Inconsistent timestamps or coordinate information.
- Disagreement between metadata and the actual data structure.
- Unusual values or potentially invalid measurements.
- Differences between original and updated data products.
- Conflicting source descriptions or quality information.

Distinguish between detectable inconsistencies and confirmed scientific errors.

Allow users to inspect relevant warnings and determine whether differences are scientifically meaningful.

Preserve original values and source information when inconsistencies are identified.

### Design Guidance

Unexpected or conflicting information should not automatically be treated as erroneous.

Differences may result from different instruments, observational conditions, processing methods, coordinate conventions, data releases or scientific interpretations.

The application should identify discrepancies where possible without silently modifying values or selecting one source as correct without justification.

## 4.8.7 External Resource Reliability

### Core Requirements

Evaluate the reliability of external resources using relevant indicators, including:

- Scientific and institutional authority.
- Resource ownership and responsible organization.
- Documentation quality and availability.
- Transparency of data collection and processing methods.
- Availability of metadata and provenance information.
- Use of established scientific standards and conventions.
- Data coverage and completeness.
- Availability of stable identifiers and persistent references.
- Evidence of continued operation and maintenance.
- Availability of technical documentation and support.
- Known limitations, inconsistencies or reliability concerns.
- Long-term sustainability and continued accessibility.

Distinguish between established authoritative resources, specialized research resources, community-maintained resources and sources whose reliability has not yet been sufficiently evaluated.

Allow reliability assessments to be revised when new information becomes available.

### Design Guidance

Resource reliability should not be determined solely by popularity, institutional reputation or the apparent professionalism of a website.

A resource may be authoritative for one type of information but unsuitable for another.

For example, a specialized observational archive may be an appropriate source for calibrated instrument data but may not provide the most suitable reference information for a particular scientific measurement.

The application should evaluate reliability in the context of the resource's intended scientific use.

## 4.8.8 External Resource Availability and Service Status

### Core Requirements

- Check the availability of supported external resources where practical.
- Identify resources that are operational, temporarily unavailable, inaccessible or discontinued when this information can be established.
- Distinguish external service failures from local application errors.
- Communicate when search, retrieval or other resource operations cannot be completed.
- Preserve information about unavailable resources when relevant to existing research materials.
- Allow users to retry failed operations where appropriate.
- Avoid repeatedly attempting operations against resources known to be unavailable without a meaningful change in conditions.
- Identify when availability information may be outdated.
- Preserve previously acquired data when an external resource becomes unavailable.

### Design Guidance

External resources are outside the application's direct control.

A temporary archive outage should not be interpreted as evidence that the underlying dataset has been deleted or invalidated.

The application should communicate the observed service status without making stronger claims than the available information supports.

Previously retrieved data and saved research should remain accessible independently of the continued availability of the original external service, subject to applicable access and retention conditions.

## 4.8.9 External Resource Changes and Data Releases

### Core Requirements

Identify relevant changes to external resources and scientific datasets where information is available.

These may include:

- Dataset updates or replacements.
- New data releases.
- Recalibrated or reprocessed observations.
- Corrected measurements or metadata.
- Changes to catalogue contents.
- Modified documentation.
- Deprecated data products.
- Relocated resources.
- Discontinued archives or databases.
- Changes that affect previously saved resource references.

Preserve information about the version or release originally used in research wherever possible.

Identify when a previously acquired resource may have been updated or superseded.

Communicate potential impacts on existing research without automatically replacing historical data or changing previous results.

Allow users to investigate updated resources and decide whether to incorporate them into ongoing work.

### Design Guidance

External scientific resources evolve over time.

An archive may release recalibrated observations, revise metadata, correct errors or replace older data products.

These changes may improve data quality, but they can also affect previously obtained results.

The application should preserve the distinction between the resource originally used and any subsequently available version.

Historical research should remain understandable even when the external source changes.

## 4.8.10 External Service Failures and Retrieval Reliability

### Core Requirements

- Identify relevant external service failures and communication problems.
- Distinguish connection failures, timeouts, authentication problems, service errors and unsupported operations where possible.
- Provide useful information about unsuccessful requests.
- Support appropriate retry behavior.
- Avoid treating incomplete responses as complete scientific data.
- Identify potentially interrupted or partially completed retrievals.
- Preserve successfully acquired information when unrelated requests fail.
- Communicate when a resource cannot be accessed through the application's available integration.
- Avoid silently substituting another source when doing so could change the scientific meaning or provenance of the requested data.
- Record significant service failures where they affect research continuity.

### Design Guidance

External services may fail independently of the application.

Failures should be handled in a way that preserves user work and makes the source of the problem understandable.

When an alternative resource is available, the application may identify it as an option, but it should not silently substitute data from a different provider or release.

The user should be able to distinguish between a failed request and a successful retrieval of incomplete or limited data.

## 4.8.11 Quality and Reliability Information Over Time

### Core Requirements

- Preserve relevant quality and reliability information associated with acquired datasets.
- Retain significant warnings and known limitations.
- Identify when a previous quality assessment may no longer reflect the current resource or dataset version.
- Allow updated quality information to be reviewed without overwriting historical assessments.
- Preserve the original provider's quality information when new assessments are generated.
- Identify when external resource changes may affect previously recorded quality information.
- Maintain relevant records of significant reliability or availability changes.
- Avoid presenting outdated assessments as current without qualification.

### Design Guidance

Quality and reliability information may change as datasets are corrected, recalibrated or supplemented and as external resources evolve.

The application should allow users to review updated information while preserving the context of previous research.

For example, a later archive release may correct a calibration issue. This should not silently change the interpretation of a dataset version used in an earlier analysis.

## General Principles

Data quality, reliability and availability should:

- Preserve original quality information and warnings.
- Communicate completeness, uncertainty, calibration and measurement limitations.
- Identify missing, invalid and inconsistent information.
- Evaluate scientific suitability in relation to the intended research activity.
- Distinguish confirmed problems from potential concerns.
- Separate provider-supplied information from application-generated assessments.
- Evaluate external resource reliability using relevant scientific and operational indicators.
- Communicate external service availability and retrieval limitations.
- Preserve historical data versions and previously acquired resources.
- Identify significant changes to external datasets and services.
- Avoid silently modifying source data or historical results.
- Make quality and reliability information accessible when users need it.

## General Principle

The application should help users understand not only what scientific data is available, but also what limitations affect it, how reliable its source appears to be and whether it is suitable for a particular research purpose.

Quality, reliability and availability should be communicated transparently, allowing users to make informed decisions without having scientific judgments silently imposed on their behalf.
