

# 3.4 — Photometry, Transits and Time-Series Analysis

## Purpose

The application should provide comprehensive capabilities for examining and analysing astronomical brightness measurements and other time-dependent observations.

Photometry and time-series analysis are important across several areas of astronomy. They support investigations of stellar variability, exoplanet transits, eclipses, periodic phenomena, changing astronomical sources and other time-dependent processes.

The application should allow users to examine photometric measurements, construct and interpret light curves, investigate temporal patterns and apply appropriate analytical methods.

These capabilities should support both exploratory examination and quantitative analysis, accommodating different instruments, observation strategies, measurement conventions and levels of data quality.

The application should also allow photometric and time-series results to contribute to broader investigations involving spectroscopy, orbital analysis, modelling and comparative research.

## 3.4.1 Photometric Data Representation and Examination

### Core Requirements

- Support examination of astronomical brightness measurements.
- Accommodate different photometric representations, including flux, relative flux, magnitude and other relevant measurement types.
- Support different photometric bands and filter systems.
- Preserve the distinction between absolute and relative measurements.
- Display measurement units and conventions where available.
- Support examination of individual measurements and selected portions of a dataset.
- Preserve relevant observational metadata and measurement uncertainties.
- Accommodate measurements collected using different instruments and observational facilities.
- Identify missing, invalid or incomplete measurements.
- Allow users to examine multiple photometric datasets within an investigation.

### Design Guidance

Photometric measurements may represent different physical quantities and use different conventions.

For example, astronomical brightness may be represented using flux measurements or logarithmic magnitude scales. These representations should not be treated as interchangeable without appropriate conversion and supporting information.

The application should make the meaning of each measurement clear and preserve relevant units, calibration information and observational context.

## 3.4.2 Light Curve Construction and Visualization

### Core Requirements

- Support construction of light curves from appropriate time-dependent photometric measurements.
- Display brightness measurements as a function of time.
- Accommodate different time representations and conventions.
- Provide appropriate axis labels, units and measurement descriptions.
- Support inspection of individual measurements and selected time intervals.
- Allow users to zoom, pan and examine specific portions of a light curve.
- Support display of measurement uncertainties where available.
- Allow multiple light curves to be displayed and compared.
- Support overlays, separate panels and other appropriate comparative presentations.
- Preserve the distinction between measured data and visual adjustments.
- Support examination of both complete light curves and selected events.
- Allow users to compare folded and unfolded light curves where appropriate.

### Design Guidance

Light curves should provide an accessible representation of how an astronomical source changes over time.

Users should be able to examine both overall temporal behaviour and individual measurements without losing the context of the original observations.

Visualization settings should not silently modify the underlying measurements or imply that visual smoothing represents additional observational evidence.

## 3.4.3 Time Systems and Temporal Data Handling

### Core Requirements

- Support relevant astronomical time representations and conventions.
- Accommodate different time standards and reference systems where required.
- Support conversion between compatible time representations.
- Preserve original timestamps and associated metadata.
- Account for time zones, reference epochs and time-scale differences where relevant.
- Support observation times expressed using appropriate astronomical standards.
- Identify missing or ambiguous time information.
- Communicate when a temporal conversion requires additional information or assumptions.
- Preserve the precision and accuracy of available timing information.

### Design Guidance

Astronomical time-series analysis can be sensitive to the time system used to record observations.

Different datasets may use different time standards, reference epochs or timing corrections. These differences must be understood before observations are combined or compared.

The application should distinguish between a simple display-format change and a scientifically meaningful time conversion.

## 3.4.4 Photometric Calibration and Corrections

### Core Requirements

Support appropriate photometric calibration and correction procedures, including where scientifically applicable:

- Instrumental magnitude correction.
- Flux calibration.
- Background and sky subtraction.
- Atmospheric extinction correction.
- Flat-field and detector-related corrections.
- Aperture corrections.
- Differential photometry.
- Normalization of relative measurements.
- Time-dependent instrumental or observational corrections.
- Other instrument-specific or observation-specific procedures.

For applicable operations:

- Identify required inputs and assumptions.
- Allow users to review relevant parameters.
- Preserve original measurements.
- Create identifiable derived outputs.
- Record the corrections and transformations applied.
- Communicate when required calibration information is unavailable.
- Distinguish calibrated measurements from uncalibrated or relative measurements.

### Design Guidance

Photometric corrections should reflect the observational method, instrument and scientific purpose.

Some investigations may use calibrated absolute measurements, while others rely on relative brightness changes.

The application should not assume that every dataset requires identical corrections or that a correction suitable for one observational method is appropriate for another.

## 3.4.5 Transit Detection and Eclipse Analysis

### Core Requirements

Support investigation and analysis of astronomical transit and eclipse observations, including:

- Exoplanet transit detection and candidate identification.
- Secondary eclipses.
- Stellar eclipsing binaries.
- Other relevant occultation and eclipse phenomena.
- Searching stellar time-series observations for potential transit signals.
- Identifying periodic reductions in stellar brightness.
- Examining individual and repeated transit or eclipse events.
- Investigating candidate orbital periods and phase-folded light curves.
- Measuring event depth, duration, ingress and egress characteristics.
- Examining transit and eclipse timing.
- Comparing observed events with theoretical models.
- Supporting analysis of incomplete, noisy or partially observed events.
- Evaluating possible false positives and alternative explanations.
- Distinguishing candidate transit signals from confirmed planetary detections.

### Design Guidance

Transit and eclipse analysis should support both exploratory investigation and quantitative parameter estimation.

The application should not rely on simplistic identification of periodic brightness reductions as proof of an exoplanet.

Candidate signals may arise from eclipsing binaries, stellar variability, instrumental effects, data-processing problems or other astrophysical phenomena.

The application should support established detection algorithms and statistical methods for evaluating potential transit signals and investigating alternative explanations.

A candidate signal should retain its candidate status until sufficient supporting evidence is available to justify a stronger conclusion.

## 3.4.6 Transit and Eclipse Modelling

### Core Requirements

- Support modelling of transit and eclipse light curves.
- Allow users to configure relevant model parameters.
- Support estimation of parameters such as:
  - Transit or eclipse depth.
  - Transit duration.
  - Orbital period.
  - Mid-transit time.
  - Planet-to-star radius ratio.
  - Orbital inclination.
  - Relevant orbital parameters.
- Accommodate relevant stellar and planetary properties.
- Support appropriate treatment of limb darkening.
- Support investigation of eccentricity-related effects where scientifically appropriate.
- Allow comparison between observed light curves and model predictions.
- Provide access to fitted parameters and associated uncertainties where available.
- Support examination of model residuals.
- Communicate model assumptions and limitations.
- Preserve original observations and distinguish model outputs from measured data.
- Allow users to investigate how changes in model parameters affect predicted light curves.

### Design Guidance

Transit and eclipse models should provide physically meaningful interpretations of observed brightness changes.

The application should make clear which parameters are directly constrained by observations and which depend on assumptions, external measurements or adopted models.

For example, transit depth can constrain the planet-to-star radius ratio under appropriate assumptions, but deriving the planetary radius also requires information about the host star.

Orbital inclination, eccentricity and other system properties may influence the interpretation of an observed transit and should be considered where relevant.

A numerical fit should not automatically be interpreted as proof that a particular physical explanation is correct.

## 3.4.7 Secondary Eclipses, Phase Curves and Planetary Light

### Core Requirements

Support investigations beyond primary transits, including:

- Secondary-eclipse observations.
- Planetary thermal emission.
- Reflected-light measurements.
- Orbital phase variations.
- Phase-curve construction and examination.
- Day-side and night-side brightness comparisons.
- Investigation of planetary brightness throughout an orbit.
- Comparison of phase-dependent observations across different epochs.
- Examination of wavelength-dependent planetary brightness where suitable observations are available.
- Investigation of atmospheric and thermal properties through observed phase-dependent behaviour.
- Comparison of observed measurements with theoretical or model-generated predictions.

### Design Guidance

Secondary eclipses and phase curves provide information about planetary emission, reflected light and changes in observable brightness throughout an orbit.

These observations may help constrain atmospheric and thermal properties, including differences between planetary day-side and night-side conditions.

The application should distinguish thermal emission from reflected-light contributions where the available observations and analytical methods permit.

These capabilities should integrate with spectroscopy and atmospheric characterization, allowing photometric and spectroscopic observations to contribute to broader planetary investigations.

## 3.4.8 Periodic Signal Detection and Period Analysis

### Core Requirements

Support investigation of periodic or approximately periodic astronomical signals through appropriate methods, including:

- Period estimation.
- Period searches.
- Frequency analysis.
- Phase folding.
- Periodogram examination.
- Identification of candidate periodic signals.
- Examination of multiple possible periods.
- Comparison of estimated periods with expected physical or orbital properties.
- Evaluation of aliases and sampling-related effects.
- Support for irregularly sampled observations where appropriate.
- Future capabilities should include Lomb–Scargle periodogram analysis for investigating approximately sinusoidal periodic signals, including stellar variability.

Allow users to examine the methods and assumptions used to identify candidate periods.

### Design Guidance

Periodic signal analysis is relevant to exoplanet transits, stellar rotation, pulsating stars, eclipsing binaries and other variable astronomical sources.

Different period-detection methods have different assumptions and limitations.

The application should avoid treating a detected periodicity as automatically establishing its physical cause. Sampling, observational gaps, aliases and other signals may produce ambiguous results.

## 3.4.9 Stellar Variability and Time-Dependent Behaviour

### Core Requirements

Support investigation of changing astronomical brightness and related temporal phenomena, including:

- Stellar variability.
- Stellar rotation-related brightness changes.
- Pulsations.
- Flares and other transient brightening events.
- Eclipsing binary variability.
- Long-term brightness trends.
- Irregular variability.
- Changes associated with stellar activity.
- Other scientifically relevant time-dependent behaviour.

Allow users to examine temporal characteristics, compare observations and apply appropriate analytical methods.

### Design Guidance

Astronomical variability may be periodic, quasi-periodic, transient, irregular or long-term.

The application should support different forms of temporal behaviour without forcing every variable source into a periodic model.

Where the physical origin of a signal is uncertain, the application should distinguish observed variability from proposed interpretations.

## 3.4.10 Time-Series Preparation and Filtering

### Core Requirements

Support appropriate preparation procedures for photometric and other time-series datasets, including:

- Data selection and filtering.
- Identification of invalid or unreliable measurements.
- Outlier examination and handling.
- Time-based sorting and organization.
- Normalization and detrending.
- Removal or modelling of appropriate background trends.
- Resampling and binning where scientifically justified.
- Treatment of irregular sampling.
- Identification of observational gaps.
- Preparation for period analysis and model fitting.

For applicable operations:

- Allow users to inspect relevant parameters.
- Preserve original observations.
- Create identifiable derived datasets.
- Record processing steps and assumptions.
- Communicate potential effects on scientific interpretation.
- Avoid automatically removing measurements without appropriate justification.

### Design Guidance

Time-series preparation can substantially affect the signals available for subsequent analysis.

For example, detrending may help reveal a transit or periodic signal, but an inappropriate procedure could also remove genuine astrophysical variability.

Preparation methods should therefore be selected according to the scientific objective and observational characteristics.

## 3.4.11 Multi-Epoch Photometry and Long-Term Stellar Observation Histories

### Core Requirements

- Support comparison of repeated observations of the same astronomical object.
- Allow examination of observations collected at different times.
- Support comparison across instruments, facilities and photometric bands.
- Allow users to examine changes in brightness over different timescales.
- Support comparison of individual events and repeated periodic phenomena.
- Accommodate differences in calibration, sensitivity and observational conditions.
- Preserve the identity, provenance and metadata of each observation.
- Identify limitations that may affect direct comparisons.
- Support cumulative observational histories for individual stars.
- Allow additional observations to be incorporated into an existing stellar research record over time.
- Support investigation of historical and recent brightness measurements, repeated photometric observations, stellar spectra, stellar properties, variability measurements and relevant planetary-system information.
- Allow different types of observations to contribute to a broader record of an individual astronomical object.
- Preserve the original identity and scientific context of each contributing dataset.
- Support investigation of changes in stellar properties and behaviour across multiple observational epochs.

### Design Guidance

Comparative photometry should support investigations involving repeated observations, multiple observational facilities and broader astronomical populations.

Long-term stellar observation histories should allow users to build increasingly comprehensive records of individual objects rather than treating every observation as an isolated dataset.

For example, a user investigating a particular star should be able to examine its historical brightness measurements alongside more recent photometry, spectra, catalogue information and other relevant observations.

The application should account for differences in instruments, observation conditions, time systems, calibration, measurement conventions and data quality before treating datasets as directly comparable.

Original observations and their provenance must remain preserved.

The persistent organization of astronomical object research records is coordinated with Section 6.4. External observation discovery and retrieval are addressed in Section 4.

## 3.4.12 Time-Series Uncertainty and Observational Limitations

### Core Requirements

- Support display and examination of measurement uncertainties.
- Preserve source-provided uncertainty information.
- Accommodate uncertainties associated with derived parameters.
- Support appropriate uncertainty propagation during analytical operations.
- Identify missing uncertainty information.
- Distinguish measured uncertainty from estimated or model-derived uncertainty.
- Communicate limitations associated with sampling, cadence, observational coverage and signal quality.
- Identify potential effects of systematic errors and instrumental variability.
- Avoid presenting results with unjustified numerical precision.
- Preserve relevant uncertainty information when results are transferred to other capabilities.
- Communicate when incomplete observations or limited signal quality prevent reliable analysis.
- Identify when a detected pattern may be influenced by observational or processing limitations.

### Design Guidance

Time-series measurements must be interpreted in the context of their uncertainty and observational coverage.

A signal may appear periodic or transient because of limited sampling, systematic effects or incomplete observations.

The application should help users evaluate these limitations rather than presenting every detected pattern as a reliable astrophysical signal.

Detailed uncertainty management, validation and scientific safeguards are addressed in Section 5.

## 3.4.13 Research into Known Planets and Published Characterizations

### Core Requirements

- Support investigation of already-discovered and characterized exoplanets.
- Allow users to examine existing planetary properties and relevant observational records.
- Support comparison of published transit and eclipse measurements with available observations.
- Allow users to investigate established planetary systems and their observational histories.
- Support comparison of observed light curves with published or independently generated models.
- Allow examination of how previous researchers derived and interpreted planetary and orbital parameters.
- Support comparison of different published measurements and scientific interpretations.
- Distinguish established findings from uncertain measurements, candidate detections and model-dependent interpretations.
- Support the use of well-characterized objects as reference cases for evaluating and validating analytical capabilities.
- Allow relevant photometric investigations to contribute to broader research into known planetary systems.

### Design Guidance

Research into existing exoplanets is an important part of developing and evaluating the application's scientific capabilities.

Well-characterized objects with substantial observational records and published research can provide valuable opportunities to examine established methods, reproduce previous analyses and investigate differences between scientific interpretations.

For example, users should be able to compare observed transit measurements with published characterizations, examine the assumptions used to estimate planetary properties and investigate whether alternative analytical approaches produce consistent results.

The application should preserve the distinction between published conclusions, independently reproduced results and new interpretations.

## 3.4.14 Integration with Other Scientific Capabilities and Analytical Outputs

### Core Requirements

- Allow photometric and time-series results to contribute to broader scientific investigations.
- Support integration with orbital and dynamical analysis.
- Allow transit and eclipse measurements to contribute to planetary characterization.
- Support comparison with spectroscopic observations.
- Allow time-dependent measurements to inform stellar and planetary modelling.
- Support integration with other observational methods where scientifically appropriate.
- Preserve metadata, units, uncertainty and provenance when transferring results.
- Distinguish observed measurements from derived parameters and model interpretations.
- Allow users to save and revisit photometric analyses and time-series results.
- Support export of light curves and processed measurements.
- Support export of fitted models, estimated parameters and relevant analytical outputs.
- Preserve measurement units, timestamps and associated metadata where supported.
- Include relevant uncertainty information.
- Distinguish original observations, prepared datasets, fitted models and derived measurements.
- Allow analytical outputs to be reused in compatible scientific capabilities.
- Preserve relationships between outputs and the observations from which they originated.

### Design Guidance

Photometry and time-series analysis should contribute to integrated scientific investigations rather than functioning as isolated activities.

For example, transit observations may be combined with stellar properties, radial velocity measurements, spectroscopic observations and orbital models to constrain the characteristics of an exoplanetary system.

The application should preserve the relationships between these different sources of evidence and make clear which conclusions depend on combined observations or model assumptions.

## General Principles

Photometry, transits and time-series analysis should:

- Support different photometric measurement types and conventions.
- Provide clear light curve visualization and interactive examination.
- Preserve accurate temporal information and time conventions.
- Support appropriate photometric calibration and correction procedures.
- Provide transit detection, candidate evaluation, eclipse and variability analysis.
- Support quantitative transit and eclipse modelling.
- Include secondary eclipses, phase curves, planetary thermal emission and reflected-light investigations.
- Support periodic signal detection and modelling.
- Accommodate irregular, incomplete and multi-epoch observations.
- Support cumulative observational histories for individual astronomical objects.
- Allow investigation of known planetary systems and published characterizations.
- Preserve uncertainty and observational limitations.
- Support comparative and interdisciplinary investigations.
- Distinguish measured observations from derived parameters and model interpretations.
- Preserve provenance and relationships between original data and analytical outputs.

## General Principle

Photometric and time-series capabilities should allow users to investigate how astronomical sources change over time while maintaining the scientific context, uncertainty and observational limitations necessary for reliable interpretation.
