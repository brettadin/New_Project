# 3.9 — Comparative, Multi-Source and Integrated Analysis

## Purpose

The application should provide scientific capabilities for comparing and integrating information from multiple observations, instruments, facilities, datasets, analytical methods and scientific sources.

Astronomical investigations frequently require more than one observation or measurement to answer a research question. Different instruments may observe the same object at different wavelengths, different facilities may provide complementary measurements, and separate analytical methods may constrain different properties of the same astronomical system.

Comparative and integrated analysis should allow users to examine these relationships while preserving the identity, scientific context, provenance, uncertainty and limitations of each contributing resource.

The application should support investigations ranging from straightforward comparisons between two observations to more complex studies involving multiple datasets, observational methods, scientific models and astronomical populations.

Integration should be guided by scientific compatibility rather than convenience. The application must not assume that datasets can be combined directly simply because they concern the same object, contain similar numerical measurements or can be displayed together.

These capabilities should connect the specialized scientific tools established throughout Section 3 and support interdisciplinary research without compromising the scientific meaning of individual observations or results.

## 3.9.1 General Comparative and Integrated Analysis Framework

### Core Requirements

- Support comparisons involving multiple observations, measurements, datasets and scientific resources.
- Allow users to investigate individual objects, complete systems and broader astronomical populations.
- Support comparisons between observations of the same object and observations of different objects.
- Accommodate data from different instruments, facilities, observational campaigns and sources.
- Support integration of complementary scientific measurements and analytical outputs.
- Allow observational data to be compared with reference information and model predictions.
- Preserve the identity, provenance and scientific context of all contributing resources.
- Maintain relevant units, measurement conventions, metadata and uncertainty.
- Identify important differences in data quality, coverage and methodological assumptions.
- Support exploratory and quantitative comparisons where scientifically appropriate.
- Allow comparative investigations to develop as additional observations or evidence become available.

### Design Guidance

Comparative analysis and integrated analysis are related but distinct activities.

**Comparative analysis** examines similarities, differences, relationships and patterns between scientific resources.

**Integrated analysis** uses compatible information from multiple resources together to investigate a broader scientific question or derive a combined result.

A comparison does not necessarily require combining datasets into a single representation. Similarly, an integrated investigation does not necessarily require merging the original data.

The application should support both approaches while preserving the distinction between examining related information and scientifically combining it.

## 3.9.2 Comparison of Multiple Observations of the Same Object

### Core Requirements

- Allow users to compare multiple observations of the same astronomical object.
- Support observations obtained at different times, wavelengths, instruments and facilities.
- Preserve individual observation identities and source information.
- Allow comparison of observations obtained during different observational campaigns.
- Support examination of changes, consistency and variability between observations.
- Allow users to compare measurements produced through different processing or calibration approaches.
- Preserve relevant differences in observational conditions and measurement conventions.
- Support identification of missing observations, incomplete coverage and differences in data quality.
- Allow users to select which observations contribute to a particular comparison.
- Maintain links between comparative results and their original observations.

### Design Guidance

Repeated observations can provide important information about an astronomical object.

For example, observations of an exoplanet obtained during different transits may be compared to investigate atmospheric consistency, temporal changes or differences between observational instruments.

Similarly, repeated stellar observations may help investigate variability, long-term behaviour and changes in measured properties.

The application should not automatically treat differences between observations as evidence of physical change. Instrumental effects, calibration differences, observational conditions and data processing may contribute to apparent differences.

## 3.9.3 Multi-Instrument and Multi-Facility Comparisons

### Core Requirements

Support comparison of observations obtained through different instruments, observatories and research facilities.

Relevant comparisons include:

- Different instruments observing the same astronomical object.
- Different instruments observing the same physical phenomenon.
- Ground-based and space-based observations.
- Observations from different observational facilities.
- Observations obtained using different detector technologies.
- Measurements obtained using different instrumental configurations.
- Data products generated through different processing pipelines.
- Independent observations of the same event or object.

Allow users to examine relevant differences in:

- Instrument sensitivity.
- Spectral or spatial resolution.
- Wavelength coverage.
- Time coverage.
- Calibration procedures.
- Measurement precision.
- Detector characteristics.
- Observational conditions.
- Data processing and reduction methods.

Preserve instrument-specific metadata and measurement characteristics.

### Design Guidance

Different instruments may provide complementary information, but their measurements are not necessarily interchangeable.

For example, two instruments may observe the same spectral feature while having different resolving power, sensitivity, wavelength calibration and instrumental response.

The application should support comparisons that account for these differences rather than assuming that the measurements can be compared directly without preparation.

## 3.9.4 Multi-Wavelength and Multi-Modal Analysis

### Core Requirements

Support scientific investigations that compare or integrate observations obtained at different wavelengths or through different observational methods.

Relevant combinations include:

- Radio and microwave observations.
- Infrared observations.
- Visible-light observations.
- Ultraviolet observations.
- X-ray observations.
- Gamma-ray observations.
- Spectroscopic and photometric measurements.
- Imaging and spectroscopic observations.
- Astrometric and radial velocity measurements.
- Time-series and spatial observations.
- Transmission and emission spectroscopy.
- Thermal emission and reflected-light measurements.

Allow users to examine complementary information provided by different observational methods.

Support comparison of observations that investigate different physical properties or processes within the same astronomical object or system.

Preserve the original measurement type, wavelength or frequency coverage, observational conditions and scientific limitations.

### Design Guidance

Multi-wavelength and multi-modal research can provide a more complete understanding of astronomical objects and physical processes.

However, observations at different wavelengths or through different methods may probe different physical regions, processes or timescales.

For example, visible-light imaging and infrared spectroscopy may provide different information about a planetary atmosphere. Their results may complement one another without representing equivalent measurements.

The application should communicate these distinctions and avoid presenting combined visualizations or analyses as though all contributing observations measure the same physical quantity.

## 3.9.5 Data Alignment, Harmonization and Compatibility

### Core Requirements

Support appropriate preparation of datasets before they are compared or integrated.

Relevant operations may include:

- Unit conversion.
- Coordinate and reference-frame transformation.
- Time-system and timestamp alignment.
- Wavelength, frequency and wavenumber conversion.
- Spectral calibration and wavelength alignment.
- Spatial registration and image alignment.
- Resolution matching where scientifically justified.
- Resampling and interpolation.
- Appropriate normalization and scaling.
- Calibration and correction procedures.
- Consistent treatment of measurement conventions.
- Selection of compatible data ranges or regions.

Allow users to inspect the original and transformed representations.

Preserve original data and maintain separate derived representations when transformations are performed.

Record the methods, parameters, assumptions and relevant software associated with data preparation.

Identify when required information is missing or when a proposed transformation is scientifically unsuitable.

### Design Guidance

Data harmonization should make meaningful comparison possible without concealing differences in the original observations.

For example, observations using different wavelength grids may require alignment or resampling before direct numerical comparison. However, resampling cannot restore information that was never measured, and matching numerical grids does not make two instruments equivalent.

Similarly, matching spatial resolution may be useful when comparing images, but the process must account for the original resolution and any resulting loss of detail.

The application should distinguish between transformations that establish a scientifically meaningful common representation and transformations that only make datasets easier to display together.

## 3.9.6 Cross-Observation and Temporal Comparisons

### Core Requirements

Support comparisons involving observations obtained at different times, epochs or stages of an astronomical event.

Relevant capabilities include:

- Comparison of repeated observations.
- Examination of long-term changes.
- Comparison of different observational epochs.
- Identification of temporal variability.
- Comparison of measurements before, during and after relevant events.
- Examination of recurring phenomena.
- Comparison of time-series observations from different sources.
- Investigation of differences between observational campaigns.
- Support for time-dependent comparisons involving multiple instruments.

Preserve the time systems, reference epochs, observation times and relevant timing uncertainties associated with contributing data.

Allow users to examine temporal differences without automatically interpreting them as physical changes.

### Design Guidance

Temporal comparisons are important in research involving variable stars, exoplanet transits, planetary atmospheres, stellar activity and transient astronomical phenomena.

Different observations may have different temporal coverage, cadence, precision and observational conditions.

The application should make these differences understandable and support appropriate temporal alignment where scientifically justified.

## 3.9.7 Comparative Analysis Across Astronomical Objects and Systems

### Core Requirements

Support comparative research involving different astronomical objects and complete systems.

Relevant comparisons include:

- Solar System planets and exoplanets.
- Different exoplanets and planetary systems.
- Different stars and stellar populations.
- Planetary and stellar atmospheres.
- Different galaxies and astronomical structures.
- Objects with similar or contrasting physical properties.
- Objects observed through different methods or instruments.
- Systems with different orbital configurations.
- Objects belonging to different environments or evolutionary stages.

Allow users to select relevant objects and properties for comparison.

Support comparisons involving measured, calculated, estimated and model-dependent properties.

Preserve the source, uncertainty and scientific context of each compared value.

Allow comparisons to incorporate relevant relationships between objects and their parent systems.

### Design Guidance

Comparative astronomy can reveal patterns and relationships that are difficult to identify through isolated investigations.

For example, researchers may compare exoplanet atmospheric properties across different planetary classes, examine relationships between host-star characteristics and planetary properties, or compare Solar System planets with exoplanets.

The application should account for differences in available evidence, observational methods, measurement uncertainty and object classification.

A comparison should not imply that two objects are physically equivalent simply because they share a broad category or have similar reported values.

## 3.9.8 Reference Data and Scientific Model Comparisons

### Core Requirements

Support comparisons between observational information and relevant scientific reference resources.

Relevant resources may include:

- Reference spectra.
- Molecular and atomic databases.
- Published astronomical measurements.
- Catalogue properties.
- Established planetary and stellar parameters.
- Laboratory measurements.
- Theoretical calculations.
- Synthetic spectra.
- Scientific model predictions.
- Published research results.

Allow users to examine the source, method and scientific context of reference information.

Support comparison between observations and reference measurements or model predictions.

Preserve distinctions between laboratory measurements, astronomical observations, theoretical predictions and derived reference values.

Allow alternative reference resources or models to be examined where appropriate.

Maintain relevant source attribution and provenance.

### Design Guidance

Reference comparisons can help identify spectral features, evaluate physical properties and assess whether observations are consistent with existing scientific knowledge.

However, reference data and astronomical observations may represent different physical conditions, measurement environments and assumptions.

For example, a laboratory molecular spectrum may provide useful reference information for interpreting an astronomical observation, but the observed atmospheric signal may also depend on temperature, pressure, abundance, radiative transfer and other physical effects.

The application should preserve these distinctions and avoid treating reference similarity as conclusive evidence of a particular interpretation.

Detailed resource discovery and source attribution are addressed in Section 4.

## 3.9.9 Integrated Analysis Across Scientific Methods

### Core Requirements

Allow compatible results from different scientific methods to contribute to a shared investigation.

Relevant combinations include:

- Spectroscopy and photometry.
- Spectroscopy and atmospheric modelling.
- Transit measurements and radial velocity measurements.
- Imaging and astrometric measurements.
- Stellar characterization and planetary characterization.
- Orbital analysis and photometric observations.
- Atmospheric measurements and theoretical predictions.
- Observational evidence and computational simulations.

Allow users to examine how different measurements constrain related physical properties.

Support integration of compatible analytical outputs without requiring the original datasets to be merged.

Preserve the relationship between each result and its contributing observations, methods, configurations and assumptions.

Identify missing information or incompatible measurement characteristics before performing an integrated operation.

Allow users to inspect the individual contributions to an integrated result.

### Design Guidance

Different scientific methods may provide complementary constraints on the same physical property or research question.

For example, transit photometry and radial velocity measurements can contribute different constraints on an exoplanet's properties. Spectroscopy may provide additional information about its atmosphere, while stellar characterization provides important context for interpreting the planetary measurements.

Integrated analysis should preserve these distinct contributions.

The application should not assume that every related measurement can be combined into one numerical result. The appropriate integration method depends on the scientific question, the characteristics of the measurements and the assumptions of the selected analytical approach.

## 3.9.10 Combined Evidence and Joint Constraints

### Core Requirements

Support scientifically appropriate methods for evaluating evidence from multiple sources or observational approaches.

Relevant capabilities may include:

- Combining independent constraints on physical parameters.
- Comparing multiple measurements of the same property.
- Evaluating consistency between independent observations.
- Investigating complementary observational constraints.
- Supporting joint parameter estimation where scientifically justified.
- Comparing alternative explanations supported by different evidence.
- Examining the contribution of individual observations to combined results.
- Preserving uncertainty and relevant correlations.
- Identifying conflicting measurements and unresolved discrepancies.
- Maintaining a record of the methods and assumptions used to derive combined constraints.

### Design Guidance

Combining evidence may improve scientific constraints, but only when the contributing measurements and their uncertainties are treated appropriately.

The application should account for correlated measurements, shared calibration uncertainties, common model assumptions and other dependencies that may affect the interpretation of combined evidence.

Independent measurements should not automatically be treated as statistically independent merely because they originate from different files, instruments or sources.

When measurements disagree, the application should allow users to examine the discrepancy rather than automatically averaging or discarding conflicting results.

## 3.9.11 Statistical and Population-Level Comparisons

### Core Requirements

Support comparative analysis involving groups or populations of astronomical objects.

Relevant capabilities include:

- Descriptive statistical analysis.
- Distribution examination.
- Comparison of population properties.
- Relationships between physical parameters.
- Correlation analysis.
- Regression and other appropriate statistical methods.
- Grouping and classification for comparative purposes.
- Examination of trends across populations.
- Identification of outliers and unusual objects.
- Comparison of different samples and selection criteria.
- Uncertainty-aware statistical comparisons where appropriate.

Allow users to define the objects, datasets, properties and criteria included in a population-level investigation.

Preserve sample selection criteria and relevant information about excluded or unavailable observations.

Communicate the limitations of statistical conclusions when sample sizes, measurement quality or observational coverage are insufficient.

### Design Guidance

Population-level research can reveal relationships that are not apparent from individual-object investigations.

For example, researchers may investigate relationships between exoplanet mass, radius, orbital distance, host-star properties and atmospheric characteristics.

However, apparent population trends may be affected by observational selection effects, detection sensitivity, incomplete catalogues, measurement uncertainty and differences in characterization methods.

The application should support examination of these limitations rather than presenting statistical relationships without their scientific context.

Correlation should not automatically be interpreted as causation.

## 3.9.12 Data Quality, Uncertainty and Scientific Compatibility

### Core Requirements

- Preserve uncertainty information from all contributing observations and measurements.
- Identify relevant differences in data quality, resolution, precision and completeness.
- Support examination of calibration differences and systematic effects.
- Identify incompatible units, conventions, coordinate systems and measurement types.
- Preserve relevant observational and processing limitations.
- Allow users to evaluate whether datasets are suitable for a proposed comparison or integration.
- Identify potential correlations between uncertainties or systematic effects.
- Communicate when a combined result depends strongly on assumptions or incomplete information.
- Avoid silently discarding measurements or uncertainty information.
- Distinguish between unavailable, invalid, excluded and unmeasured information.

### Design Guidance

Comparative and integrated analysis must account for the scientific characteristics of contributing data.

A dataset with higher numerical precision is not necessarily more reliable if it contains unrecognized systematic effects. Similarly, a large sample may not provide a representative view of an astronomical population if its selection criteria introduce substantial bias.

The application should help users understand the limitations of comparisons and identify when further observations, preparation or methodological evaluation may be necessary.

## 3.9.13 Integrated Results and Scientific Interpretation

### Core Requirements

- Allow users to examine results produced through comparative and integrated analysis.
- Preserve relationships between integrated results and contributing datasets.
- Identify the analytical methods and assumptions used.
- Support examination of differences, trends, agreements and disagreements between contributing sources.
- Allow users to compare alternative interpretations.
- Preserve uncertainty and limitations associated with integrated results.
- Distinguish direct observations from derived results, model-dependent estimates and interpretations.
- Allow results to contribute to broader research investigations and subsequent analyses.
- Support revision of interpretations when new observations or evidence become available.

### Design Guidance

Integrated results should remain traceable to the evidence and methods that produced them.

The application should not hide disagreements between contributing sources or present a combined interpretation as more certain than its supporting evidence allows.

Users should be able to examine how individual observations, datasets and analytical methods contributed to a result.

Scientific interpretation and the acceptance of conclusions should remain subject to the integrity, uncertainty and validation requirements established in Section 5.

## 3.9.14 Comparative Visualization and Presentation

### Core Requirements

Support visualization of comparative and integrated scientific information through the general visualization capabilities established in Section 3.10.

Relevant capabilities include:

- Side-by-side comparison of observations.
- Overlaying compatible datasets.
- Multi-wavelength comparisons.
- Comparison of spectra and time-series measurements.
- Comparison of images and spatial observations.
- Observed-versus-modelled comparisons.
- Multi-object property comparisons.
- Population distributions and statistical relationships.
- Visualization of differences between observations.
- Examination of uncertainty and measurement coverage.
- Presentation of integrated results alongside their contributing evidence.

Allow users to select relevant observations, datasets, objects and analytical outputs for comparison.

Provide appropriate labels, units, legends, metadata and source information.

Distinguish visual alignment or normalization from transformations that change the scientific representation of the underlying data.

### Design Guidance

Visualization can make relationships between different observations and scientific results easier to examine.

However, visual similarity does not establish scientific equivalence.

The application should make important differences in units, scales, normalization, resolution and measurement conventions clear.

Comparative visualizations should complement the underlying data and analytical methods rather than replace them.

Detailed graphing, visualization and presentation requirements are established in Section 3.10.

## 3.9.15 Reproducibility and Comparative Analysis Records

### Core Requirements

- Preserve the identity of all datasets and observations used in comparisons.
- Record relevant source information, provenance and metadata.
- Preserve data selection criteria and any excluded observations where appropriate.
- Record transformations, conversions and harmonization procedures.
- Preserve analytical methods, configurations and assumptions.
- Maintain relationships between original data, derived representations and comparative results.
- Allow users to revisit previous comparisons and integrated analyses.
- Support comparison of results produced using alternative datasets or methods.
- Identify when contributing data or reference resources have been updated or become unavailable.
- Preserve sufficient information to understand and reproduce comparative operations where technically possible.

### Design Guidance

Comparative and integrated analyses often depend on multiple resources and preparation steps.

Their scientific value depends on being able to identify which observations contributed to the result and how they were treated.

The application should preserve these relationships without unnecessarily duplicating large underlying datasets.

Detailed analysis-level traceability and reproducibility requirements are established in Section 5.5. Project-level organization and continuity are addressed in Section 6.4.

## 3.9.16 Future Comparative and Integrated Capabilities

### Core Requirements

The application should remain extensible to additional comparative and integrated research methods as scientific capabilities develop.

Potential future areas include:

- More advanced multi-modal data integration.
- Joint statistical and computational inference.
- Population-level modelling.
- Large-scale comparative astronomical studies.
- Advanced multi-instrument data harmonization.
- Automated identification of complementary observations.
- More extensive integration of observational and theoretical resources.
- Advanced cross-disciplinary analytical workflows.
- Additional methods for investigating relationships between astronomical populations and physical processes.

### Design Guidance

More advanced integration methods may require specialized statistical approaches, computational resources and domain-specific validation.

They should be evaluated according to scientific value, methodological suitability, data availability and practical implementation requirements.

The application should establish a flexible foundation for integrated research without requiring every possible method to be implemented immediately.

## General Principles

Comparative, multi-source and integrated analysis should:

- Support comparison of multiple observations, datasets, instruments and scientific sources.
- Accommodate observations of the same object and comparisons between different objects and systems.
- Support multi-wavelength and multi-modal investigations.
- Preserve individual observation identities, provenance and scientific context.
- Provide appropriate data alignment, harmonization and compatibility assessment.
- Integrate complementary observational methods and scientific models where justified.
- Support combined evidence and joint constraints without assuming measurements are independent.
- Accommodate statistical and population-level research.
- Preserve uncertainty, data quality and methodological limitations.
- Distinguish comparison, integration and interpretation.
- Support scientifically meaningful comparative visualization.
- Preserve the relationships between contributing resources and resulting analyses.
- Remain extensible to additional comparative and integrated methods.

## General Principle

Comparative and integrated analysis should allow users to investigate astronomical objects and scientific questions through multiple sources of evidence while preserving the identity, context, uncertainty and scientific validity of every contributing observation, dataset and method.

## Implementation Boundary

This subsection establishes the intended scientific capabilities for comparative, multi-source and integrated analysis.

It does not prescribe specific statistical algorithms, data-fusion methods, database structures, software libraries, integration mechanisms or implementation technologies.

The scientific suitability of any comparison or integration method must be evaluated according to the contributing data, measurement characteristics, assumptions and research objectives.
