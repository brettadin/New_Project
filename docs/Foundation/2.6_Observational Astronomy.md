

# 2.6 — Observational Astronomy

## Purpose

Observational astronomy is a fundamental part of the application's scientific scope. It encompasses the acquisition, examination and scientific interpretation of information obtained through observations of astronomical objects and phenomena.

Observations provide much of the evidence used to investigate planets, exoplanets, stars, galaxies and other astronomical subjects. They may involve different instruments, observatories, wavelength regions, measurement techniques and observational conditions.

The application should accommodate a broad range of observational astronomy while recognizing that different observations provide different types of information and have distinct scientific limitations.

Observational astronomy should function as a cross-disciplinary research area rather than being restricted to a single category of astronomical objects.

## 2.6.1 Overall Observational Scope

### Core Requirements

- Include observational astronomy as a major scientific area within the application.
- Accommodate observations of different astronomical objects, systems and phenomena.
- Support research involving ground-based and space-based observations.
- Recognize observations obtained through different instruments, facilities and observational techniques.
- Accommodate individual observations, repeated observations and larger observational datasets.
- Support research involving observations across different wavelength regions.
- Recognize both direct observational measurements and quantities derived from observational evidence.
- Preserve the scientific context associated with each observation.

### Design Guidance

Observational astronomy should connect naturally with the other scientific disciplines defined in Section 2.

For example, observations of an exoplanet may contribute to planetary characterization, atmospheric chemistry, stellar astronomy and comparative planetary science.

The application should not treat observations as independent files without their associated scientific context, targets, instruments, measurement conditions and provenance.

## 2.6.2 Electromagnetic Spectrum and Wavelength Regions

### Core Requirements

Accommodate astronomical research involving the major regions of the electromagnetic spectrum, including:

- Radio.
- Microwave.
- Infrared.
- Visible and optical.
- Ultraviolet.
- X-ray.
- Gamma-ray.

Recognize that different wavelength regions reveal different physical processes and characteristics of astronomical objects.

Support the scientific context of observations involving multiple wavelength regions, including comparisons and combined investigations.

### Design Guidance

The application should not assume that all astronomical observations use wavelength as their primary measurement axis.

Depending on the instrument and scientific context, observations may be represented using wavelength, frequency, energy, wavenumber or other relevant quantities.

Different spectral conventions and units should retain their original meaning and measurement context.

Multi-wavelength research should preserve the relationships between observations while accounting for differences in instrument characteristics, resolution, sensitivity, timing and calibration.

## 2.6.3 Spectroscopic Observations

### Core Requirements

Recognize spectroscopy as a major observational method across astronomical research.

Include investigations involving:

- Absorption spectra.
- Emission spectra.
- Transmission spectra.
- Reflection spectra.
- Thermal and other emission measurements.
- Molecular and atomic spectral features.
- Stellar spectra.
- Planetary and exoplanetary atmospheric spectra.
- Spectral line profiles and continuum characteristics.
- Time-dependent and variable spectra.

Accommodate spectroscopic observations obtained across different wavelength regions and instrument configurations.

### Design Guidance

Spectroscopy provides information about chemical composition, physical conditions, temperature, motion and other characteristics of astronomical objects.

The scientific interpretation of a spectrum depends on its observational context, calibration, resolution, measurement conventions and relevant uncertainties.

The application should preserve distinctions between measured spectral signals, identified features, proposed chemical or physical interpretations and derived scientific results.

Detailed spectral analysis capabilities are established in Section 3.

## 2.6.4 Photometric and Brightness Observations

### Core Requirements

Include observational research involving the measurement of astronomical brightness and changes in brightness.

Relevant subjects include:

- Apparent brightness and flux measurements.
- Magnitudes and photometric systems.
- Stellar and planetary brightness variations.
- Transit light curves.
- Secondary eclipse measurements.
- Phase curves.
- Variable stars.
- Eclipsing binary systems.
- Time-dependent photometric observations.
- Multi-band and multi-wavelength photometry.

Recognize that photometric measurements may use different filters, passbands, calibration conventions and measurement systems.

### Design Guidance

Photometric observations should retain information about their measurement systems and observational conditions.

Brightness measurements obtained using different filters or instruments should not automatically be treated as directly comparable without considering their respective characteristics.

Photometry should connect with exoplanet science, stellar astronomy, planetary research and other relevant observational subjects.

## 2.6.5 Astronomical Imaging

### Core Requirements

Recognize astronomical imaging as an important observational method.

Include research involving:

- Optical and infrared images.
- Radio and other non-optical imaging.
- Planetary and lunar surface images.
- Stellar fields and star clusters.
- Nebulae and star-forming regions.
- Galaxies and other extended astronomical objects.
- Time-dependent imaging.
- Multi-wavelength image comparisons.
- Spatially resolved observations.

Accommodate differences in image resolution, scale, orientation, coordinate systems, instrument characteristics and observational conditions.

### Design Guidance

Astronomical images provide spatial information that may not be available through spectroscopy or photometry alone.

The application should recognize the scientific distinction between raw observations, calibrated images, processed images and derived measurements.

Image interpretation should retain relevant processing history and observational limitations.

Detailed image-processing and analysis functionality is addressed in Section 3.

## 2.6.6 Time-Series and Repeated Observations

### Core Requirements

Support research involving observations collected over time.

Relevant subjects include:

- Repeated observations of the same astronomical target.
- Periodic and irregular variability.
- Transit and eclipse observations.
- Stellar activity and variability.
- Rotational and orbital periods.
- Transient astronomical phenomena.
- Long-term monitoring.
- Time-dependent spectral and photometric measurements.
- Comparisons between observations obtained at different times.

Recognize the importance of observation timing, temporal resolution, sampling and time conventions.

### Design Guidance

Time-series research should preserve the chronological and observational context of measurements.

Observations collected at different times may reflect physical changes in an astronomical object, differences in observing conditions or differences in instrumentation.

These possibilities should remain distinguishable during scientific interpretation.

## 2.6.7 Ground-Based and Space-Based Observations

### Core Requirements

Accommodate observations originating from both ground-based and space-based facilities.

Recognize relevant differences involving:

- Atmospheric interference and transparency.
- Atmospheric absorption and emission.
- Seeing conditions and image resolution.
- Instrument sensitivity and wavelength coverage.
- Observational location and viewing geometry.
- Instrument calibration and operational characteristics.
- Spacecraft and telescope observing conditions.
- Ground-based observatory environments.
- Scheduling and observational constraints.

Support research involving observations from multiple facilities where scientifically appropriate.

### Design Guidance

Ground-based and space-based observations may provide complementary information, but their measurement conditions and limitations can differ substantially.

The application should preserve the origin and observational context of each dataset rather than treating all observations as equivalent.

## 2.6.8 Instruments, Observatories and Observational Facilities

### Core Requirements

Recognize the role of instruments and facilities in producing astronomical observations.

Accommodate observations associated with:

- Ground-based telescopes.
- Space telescopes.
- Radio observatories.
- Spectrographs.
- Photometric instruments.
- Imaging systems.
- Interferometric facilities.
- Other specialized astronomical instruments.

Preserve relevant instrument and facility information where available, including:

- Instrument and facility identifiers.
- Observing configuration.
- Detector or instrument characteristics.
- Spectral or spatial resolution.
- Wavelength coverage.
- Calibration information.
- Observation dates and conditions.

### Design Guidance

Instrument information is part of the scientific context of an observation.

The same astronomical target may be observed using different instruments, configurations and facilities. These differences can influence the resulting measurements and their interpretation.

The application should retain relevant instrument information without requiring every instrument to have a dedicated integration or specialized workflow.

## 2.6.9 Multi-Instrument and Multi-Wavelength Research

### Core Requirements

Support observational investigations that combine or compare data from different instruments, facilities and wavelength regions.

Relevant research subjects include:

- Multi-wavelength observations of the same target.
- Comparisons between different instruments.
- Combined spectroscopic and photometric investigations.
- Observations collected at different times.
- Ground-based and space-based observational comparisons.
- Complementary observations of the same astronomical phenomenon.
- Relationships between different observational measurements.

### Design Guidance

Multi-instrument research should account for differences in measurement conventions, calibration, resolution, sensitivity, timing and observational coverage.

Combining observations should not imply that their original measurement characteristics can be ignored.

The application should preserve the identity and provenance of individual observations while allowing scientifically appropriate comparisons and integrated investigations.

## 2.6.10 Observational Limitations and Scientific Context

### Core Requirements

Recognize that astronomical observations are subject to limitations that influence their scientific interpretation.

Relevant considerations include:

- Measurement uncertainty.
- Instrument sensitivity and detection limits.
- Spectral, temporal and spatial resolution.
- Calibration accuracy.
- Background signals and interference.
- Noise and systematic effects.
- Atmospheric and environmental conditions.
- Observational coverage and sampling.
- Selection effects and incomplete measurements.
- Differences between direct observations and inferred properties.

### Design Guidance

Observational data should not be presented as inherently complete or free from limitations.

The application should preserve available uncertainty and quality information and make relevant observational limitations accessible during research.

Missing information should not be silently replaced with assumed values or presented as measured evidence.

Scientific conclusions should reflect the quality and limitations of the observations supporting them.

## 2.6.11 Interdisciplinary Relationships

### Core Requirements

Recognize observational astronomy as a shared foundation across the application's scientific disciplines.

Support its relationship with:

- Exoplanet science.
- Planetary science and Solar System research.
- Stellar astronomy.
- Galactic and extragalactic astronomy.
- Astrophysics.
- Spectroscopy and molecular science.
- Photometry and time-domain astronomy.
- Planetary geology and other observational sciences.

Allow observations to contribute to multiple research subjects and investigations where scientifically appropriate.

### Design Guidance

Observational astronomy should not become an isolated scientific category that separates observations from the research questions they help answer.

For example, a stellar spectrum may contribute to stellar classification, chemical abundance research and the characterization of an exoplanet host star.

The application should preserve these relationships without requiring users to duplicate observations or artificially separate connected research.

## General Principles

Observational astronomy should:

- Accommodate a broad range of astronomical observations and observational methods.
- Support research across the electromagnetic spectrum.
- Recognize spectroscopy, photometry, imaging and time-series observations as important research areas.
- Accommodate ground-based and space-based observations.
- Preserve relationships between observations, instruments, facilities and astronomical targets.
- Support multi-instrument and multi-wavelength research.
- Preserve observational provenance, measurement conventions and uncertainty.
- Recognize differences in observational quality, limitations and scientific suitability.
- Connect observations naturally with the application's other scientific disciplines.
- Avoid assuming that different observational datasets are automatically interchangeable or directly comparable.

## General Principle

Observational astronomy should provide a common scientific foundation through which astronomical measurements and observations contribute to research across different objects, disciplines and wavelength regions.

## Implementation Boundary

This subsection establishes the observational subjects, methods and scientific contexts that fall within the application's scope.

It does not require every wavelength region, instrument, observatory, observational technique or data type to be supported in the initial implementation.

Specific capabilities for importing, examining, processing, analyzing, comparing and visualizing observational data are defined elsewhere in the Foundations documentation, particularly Section 3.

Data discovery, external archives, data sources, retrieval and resource integration are addressed separately in Section 4.
