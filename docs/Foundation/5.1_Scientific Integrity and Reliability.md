

# 5.1 — Scientific Integrity and Reliability

## Purpose

The application must maintain scientific integrity throughout its functionality, research workflows, data handling, analysis and presentation of results.

Scientific reliability should be a foundational requirement rather than an optional feature or an additional validation step applied only to selected tools.

The application should support established scientific methods while allowing users to explore new approaches, investigate uncertain results and develop independent interpretations. It must distinguish genuine observations from processed measurements, derived values, theoretical predictions, simulations, estimates and AI-generated content.

Scientific information should be presented honestly and transparently, with appropriate context regarding its source, reliability, assumptions and limitations.

The application should never prioritize convenience, visual presentation or apparent certainty over scientific accuracy.

## 5.1.1 Scientific Accuracy and Evidence-Based Research

### Core Requirements

- Prioritize scientific accuracy across all application functionality.
- Support established scientific principles, accepted methodologies and appropriately documented analytical approaches.
- Distinguish established scientific knowledge from hypotheses, interpretations, predictions and unresolved scientific questions.
- Avoid presenting unsupported assumptions as established scientific facts.
- Ensure that scientific calculations and analytical operations follow their stated mathematical and methodological requirements.
- Preserve the distinction between measured values, calculated quantities, inferred properties and estimated parameters.
- Identify when scientific information is incomplete, uncertain or dependent on assumptions.
- Avoid presenting approximate or simplified methods as equivalent to more rigorous scientific approaches.
- Allow users to inspect the methods and assumptions underlying scientific results.
- Support the use of appropriate scientific references and established datasets when evaluating results.

### Design Guidance

Scientific accuracy should take precedence over producing convenient or visually convincing results.

The application should not hide methodological limitations simply because an operation produces a numerical output or appears successful.

For example, a calculated planetary temperature should be identified according to the assumptions used to derive it. It should not automatically be presented as a directly measured atmospheric temperature.

## 5.1.2 Distinction Between Scientific Evidence and Generated Information

### Core Requirements

- Clearly distinguish genuine observational data from simulated, synthetic, illustrative and artificially generated data.
- Distinguish original measurements from processed data and derived analytical outputs.
- Identify theoretical predictions, model-generated results and estimated values.
- Identify AI-generated explanations, recommendations and interpretations.
- Avoid presenting generated or simulated information as genuine observational evidence.
- Preserve the origin and scientific status of information as it moves between application workspaces and research activities.
- Make the evidential status of scientific information understandable to users.
- Identify when an example dataset or illustrative visualization does not represent an actual astronomical observation.
- Avoid mixing generated and observational information without clearly identifying their different origins.
- Preserve the distinction between scientific evidence and explanatory material.

### Design Guidance

Different kinds of scientific information may appear similar when presented in a graph, table or visualization.

Their origins and evidential status must therefore remain clear.

For example, a simulated transmission spectrum may resemble an observed exoplanet spectrum, but the two have fundamentally different scientific roles.

The application should identify these differences rather than relying on users to infer them from context.

## 5.1.3 Scientific Methods and Methodological Transparency

### Core Requirements

- Use scientifically appropriate methods for the intended data and research question.
- Identify the methods applied during processing, analysis, modelling and interpretation.
- Preserve relevant assumptions, parameters, configurations and methodological choices.
- Communicate when an operation uses an approximation, simplified model or method with known limitations.
- Distinguish established methods from experimental, emerging or user-defined approaches.
- Allow users to inspect relevant methodological information.
- Avoid silently substituting one scientific method for another.
- Identify when a selected method may be unsuitable for the available data or research objective.
- Preserve information about the conditions under which a method is appropriate.
- Support comparison between alternative methods when scientifically useful.

### Design Guidance

A method producing a result does not necessarily mean that the method was scientifically appropriate for the task.

For example, applying a fitting procedure to an observational dataset may produce numerical parameters even when the dataset does not adequately support the assumptions of that procedure.

The application should help users understand whether a method is suitable, what assumptions it makes and how those assumptions may affect the result.

## 5.1.4 Scientific Data Preservation and Integrity

### Core Requirements

- Preserve original observational data and source materials.
- Avoid silently overwriting or destructively modifying original scientific datasets.
- Distinguish original data from processed, transformed and derived representations.
- Preserve relevant units, metadata, measurement conventions and uncertainty information.
- Identify when data has been modified, converted, filtered or otherwise transformed.
- Maintain relationships between source data and dependent analytical results.
- Avoid silently assigning unknown scientific metadata or measurement units.
- Distinguish missing information from measured zero values and other valid numerical results.
- Identify incomplete, corrupted or potentially unreliable data.
- Preserve the ability to revisit original observations and previous processing states.

### Design Guidance

Scientific data should remain traceable to its original source and condition.

Processing and transformation may be necessary for scientific analysis, but these operations should not destroy the information required to understand or reproduce the original measurements.

These requirements apply throughout data import, preparation, processing and analysis, including operations performed automatically.

## 5.1.5 Uncertainty, Limitations and Scientific Confidence

### Core Requirements

- Recognize uncertainty as an essential part of scientific information.
- Preserve available measurement uncertainties and relevant uncertainty metadata.
- Distinguish measurement uncertainty from model uncertainty, statistical uncertainty and uncertainty introduced through assumptions or processing.
- Identify limitations affecting the reliability or interpretation of results.
- Avoid presenting uncertain measurements or conclusions with unjustified precision.
- Distinguish confidence in a numerical result from confidence in its scientific interpretation.
- Communicate when uncertainty information is unavailable or cannot be reliably determined.
- Avoid treating the absence of reported uncertainty as evidence that a result is exact.
- Support appropriate consideration of uncertainty when comparing observations, models and reference information.
- Allow users to inspect the factors contributing to uncertainty where practical.

### Design Guidance

Uncertainty should not be treated as a minor warning attached to otherwise definitive results.

It is part of the scientific meaning of measurements, calculations and interpretations.

For example, two spectral measurements may appear to have different peak intensities, but their difference may not be scientifically meaningful when measurement uncertainty is considered.

The application should encourage interpretations that account for uncertainty rather than emphasizing numerical differences without appropriate context.

## 5.1.6 Scientific References and Supporting Evidence

### Core Requirements

- Support appropriate use of scientific publications, reference databases, observational archives and established research resources.
- Preserve available source information for scientific facts, measurements and reference values.
- Distinguish sourced information from user-provided interpretations and application-generated calculations.
- Identify when a scientific statement or reference value lacks a verifiable source.
- Preserve relevant citations and attribution information.
- Allow users to inspect supporting references where available.
- Avoid fabricating publications, citations, datasets or scientific references.
- Identify when reference information is outdated, incomplete or potentially inconsistent with newer findings.
- Support comparison with established scientific results and published research.
- Avoid treating a single reference or publication as definitive when the subject remains scientifically disputed.

### Design Guidance

Scientific statements and reference values should be traceable to appropriate supporting information whenever practical.

The application should not invent references to make an explanation appear authoritative.

Where information cannot be verified, this limitation should be communicated rather than concealed.

## 5.1.7 Reproducibility and Research Transparency

### Core Requirements

- Support research activities that can be understood and reproduced.
- Preserve relevant analytical methods, parameters, assumptions and processing configurations.
- Maintain records of significant scientific operations and decisions.
- Preserve relationships between source datasets, processing activities, analytical results and conclusions.
- Allow users to revisit previous analytical states.
- Identify relevant differences between repeated analyses.
- Avoid silently changing historical results when methods, configurations or reference resources are updated.
- Support appropriate documentation of manual and automated operations.
- Make relevant methodological information accessible when reviewing or sharing research.
- Coordinate reproducibility requirements with the detailed analysis-recording system established in Section 5.5.

### Design Guidance

Reproducibility requires more than retaining a final numerical result or visualization.

Users should be able to understand how the result was obtained, what information was used and which assumptions or processing operations influenced it.

The application should preserve sufficient context to make historical research meaningful even as its capabilities evolve.

## 5.1.8 Scientific Errors, Conflicts and Unsupported Results

### Core Requirements

- Identify and communicate detectable scientific or data-related errors.
- Distinguish computational success from scientific validity.
- Identify when results conflict with available reference information or established expectations.
- Avoid silently suppressing unusual or contradictory results.
- Allow scientifically unexpected findings to be investigated rather than automatically rejected.
- Distinguish potential errors from legitimate scientific anomalies.
- Communicate when an analytical result cannot be verified.
- Identify when insufficient data or incompatible assumptions prevent reliable interpretation.
- Avoid presenting unsupported conclusions simply because an analysis completed successfully.
- Preserve relevant warnings and unresolved issues alongside affected results.

### Design Guidance

Scientific anomalies should not automatically be treated as errors.

An unexpected observation may indicate a data problem, an unsuitable analytical method or a potentially interesting scientific result.

The application should help users investigate these possibilities without prematurely deciding which explanation is correct.

## 5.1.9 Scientific Integrity Across Automation and AI

### Core Requirements

- Apply scientific integrity requirements consistently to manual, automated and AI-assisted operations.
- Ensure automated processing does not silently introduce unsupported assumptions or alter scientific meaning.
- Identify when recommendations, interpretations or conclusions were generated by AI.
- Distinguish AI-generated suggestions from verified scientific findings.
- Avoid presenting AI-generated information as independently validated evidence.
- Preserve relevant methods, inputs and configurations associated with automated scientific operations.
- Allow users to inspect consequential automated decisions.
- Communicate when automated or AI-assisted results require further scientific verification.
- Avoid allowing AI systems to fabricate observational measurements, scientific references or research findings.
- Maintain user control over consequential scientific interpretations and decisions.

### Design Guidance

Automation and AI can improve the efficiency and accessibility of scientific research, but they must not weaken the standards applied to scientific evidence.

An AI-generated interpretation may provide a useful hypothesis or suggest an alternative explanation. It should not be treated as a confirmed scientific conclusion without appropriate supporting evidence and validation.

The same scientific standards should apply regardless of whether an operation is performed manually, automatically or with AI assistance.

## 5.1.10 Scientific Presentation and Communication

### Core Requirements

- Present scientific information accurately and with appropriate context.
- Distinguish observations, measurements, derived values, model outputs, predictions and interpretations.
- Use scientifically appropriate terminology, units, notation and numerical precision.
- Avoid visualizations or summaries that create misleading impressions.
- Communicate important assumptions, uncertainties and limitations alongside relevant results.
- Distinguish established findings from preliminary or exploratory interpretations.
- Avoid presenting correlation as proof of causation.
- Avoid implying scientific certainty where the evidence does not justify it.
- Ensure that simplified explanations do not materially misrepresent scientific concepts.
- Support clear communication of scientific findings to users with different levels of experience.

### Design Guidance

Scientific presentation should make complex information understandable without removing important scientific qualifications.

For example, a visualization may emphasize a detected spectral feature, but should not imply that the associated molecule has been conclusively identified when the available evidence supports only a possible match.

The application should balance clarity and accessibility with appropriate scientific precision.

## General Principles

Scientific integrity and reliability should:

- Prioritize scientific accuracy over convenience or presentation.
- Preserve the distinction between observations, measurements, calculations, predictions, simulations and interpretations.
- Maintain transparent and scientifically appropriate methodologies.
- Protect original scientific data and preserve its provenance.
- Communicate uncertainty, assumptions and limitations.
- Support appropriate use of scientific references and supporting evidence.
- Encourage reproducible and traceable research.
- Distinguish computational success from scientific validation.
- Allow unexpected and contradictory findings to be investigated.
- Apply consistent scientific standards to manual, automated and AI-assisted operations.
- Present scientific information honestly and without unjustified certainty.
- Preserve scientific meaning throughout the application.

## General Principle

The application must never sacrifice scientific integrity for convenience, automation, accessibility or the appearance of certainty.

Every scientific capability should support research that is transparent, appropriately qualified, evidence-based and open to critical evaluation.
