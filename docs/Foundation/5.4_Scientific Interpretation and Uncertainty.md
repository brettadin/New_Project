# 5.4 — Scientific Interpretation and Uncertainty

## Purpose

The application should support scientifically rigorous interpretation of observations, measurements, analytical results and model outputs while preserving the distinction between observational evidence, analytical findings, theoretical explanations and scientific conclusions.

Astronomical research frequently involves incomplete observations, uncertain measurements, competing explanations and results that depend on theoretical models or methodological assumptions.

The application should help users evaluate evidence, identify uncertainty and limitations, compare alternative interpretations and understand the assumptions underlying scientific conclusions.

It must not present uncertain interpretations as established facts or imply that a result is more reliable than the available evidence justifies.

Scientific interpretation should remain transparent, evidence-driven and open to revision as additional observations, improved methods or new scientific understanding become available.

## 5.4.1 Distinguishing Observations, Measurements and Interpretations

### Core Requirements

- Clearly distinguish direct observational data from measurements derived through processing or analysis.
- Distinguish measured quantities from inferred physical properties.
- Identify when results depend on theoretical models, assumptions or computational predictions.
- Distinguish scientific interpretations from established observational findings.
- Identify hypotheses and proposed explanations as such.
- Preserve relationships between interpretations and the evidence supporting them.
- Avoid presenting model-derived quantities as direct measurements.
- Make the origin and scientific status of important results understandable.
- Allow users to inspect the evidence underlying an interpretation.
- Preserve the distinction between observations, analytical outputs and scientific conclusions throughout the application.
- Ensure evidential classifications remain understandable in relevant visualizations, reports and exported results.

### Design Guidance

Scientific information can pass through several stages before supporting an interpretation.

For example, an astronomical spectrum may contain directly observed detector measurements. Calibration and processing produce a scientifically usable spectrum, while subsequent analysis may estimate spectral features or atmospheric properties.

An interpretation of the planet's atmospheric composition may then depend on comparison with theoretical models and reference spectra.

The application should preserve these distinctions so users can understand which conclusions are directly supported by observations and which depend on additional analytical or theoretical assumptions.

## 5.4.2 Uncertainty Representation and Communication

### Core Requirements

- Preserve uncertainty information supplied with original datasets.
- Support uncertainty associated with measurements, derived quantities, fitted parameters and model predictions.
- Distinguish different sources and types of uncertainty where information is available.
- Support appropriate numerical and visual representations of uncertainty.
- Identify when uncertainty information is unavailable or incomplete.
- Avoid silently assuming that measurements or analytical results have negligible uncertainty.
- Preserve uncertainty through processing and analytical workflows where scientifically appropriate.
- Identify when transformations may alter or introduce uncertainty.
- Communicate uncertainty using scientifically appropriate units, conventions and significant figures.
- Avoid presenting numerical precision beyond what the available evidence supports.
- Ensure uncertainty information remains associated with the relevant results when they are compared, summarized or exported.

### Design Guidance

Uncertainty is part of the scientific meaning of a result, not merely supplementary information.

For example, a measured planetary radius may have an associated uncertainty, while an atmospheric abundance estimate may have additional uncertainty arising from model assumptions, parameter degeneracies and observational limitations.

The application should preserve these distinctions rather than reducing all uncertainty to a single unexplained confidence value.

## 5.4.3 Sources and Types of Uncertainty

### Core Requirements

- Accommodate relevant sources of uncertainty, including:
  - Measurement uncertainty.
  - Instrumental and calibration uncertainty.
  - Observational noise and background interference.
  - Sampling and resolution limitations.
  - Data processing and transformation uncertainty.
  - Model and parameter uncertainty.
  - Statistical uncertainty.
  - Systematic uncertainty.
  - Uncertainty arising from incomplete or missing information.
- Distinguish statistical and systematic uncertainty where appropriate.
- Preserve known uncertainty sources and their associated information.
- Identify when uncertainty estimates rely on assumptions or approximations.
- Avoid combining different uncertainty sources without an appropriate scientific basis.
- Allow users to inspect relevant uncertainty information.
- Communicate when uncertainty cannot be quantified reliably.

### Design Guidance

Different sources of uncertainty can affect scientific results in different ways.

For example, observational noise may affect the precision of a measured spectral feature, while uncertainty in an instrument's calibration may introduce systematic effects across an entire dataset.

Model assumptions may introduce further uncertainty when observations are used to infer physical properties.

The application should support these distinctions and avoid implying that a single numerical uncertainty captures every limitation.

## 5.4.4 Confidence, Reliability and Evidential Strength

### Core Requirements

- Communicate the strength of evidence supporting scientific results.
- Distinguish numerical uncertainty from broader scientific confidence.
- Avoid treating statistical significance as equivalent to scientific certainty.
- Identify important limitations affecting the reliability of an interpretation.
- Support appropriate confidence or evidential assessments when scientifically justified.
- Explain the basis of qualitative confidence assessments where provided.
- Distinguish established findings from preliminary, tentative or speculative interpretations.
- Avoid assigning arbitrary confidence scores without a defined scientific basis.
- Preserve relevant supporting and contradictory evidence.
- Allow users to evaluate the evidence independently.
- Avoid presenting a single confidence classification as a substitute for explaining important sources of uncertainty and limitations.

### Design Guidance

A result can have a small reported statistical uncertainty while still being affected by systematic errors, incomplete models or other important limitations.

Likewise, a preliminary interpretation may be scientifically plausible without being sufficiently supported to constitute an established finding.

The application should avoid reducing scientific confidence to a single universal score.

Where confidence classifications are used, their meaning and basis should be clear.

## 5.4.5 Model Assumptions and Methodological Limitations

### Core Requirements

- Identify important assumptions underlying scientific models and analytical methods.
- Preserve relevant model configurations and parameter constraints.
- Communicate limitations associated with selected methods.
- Distinguish model assumptions from observational evidence.
- Identify when a model may not adequately represent the available data.
- Support inspection of relevant alternative assumptions or configurations.
- Communicate when results are sensitive to model selection or parameter choices.
- Avoid presenting model agreement as proof that a model uniquely explains the observations.
- Preserve information about the methods used to produce model-dependent results.
- Coordinate model-specific requirements with Section 3.7.
- Allow users to investigate how scientifically relevant assumptions and parameter choices affect an interpretation where the underlying method supports such investigation.

### Design Guidance

Scientific interpretations often depend on models that simplify complex physical systems.

For example, an exoplanet atmospheric retrieval may produce a plausible atmospheric composition under a particular set of assumptions about temperature structure, molecular abundances, clouds and atmospheric opacity.

Different assumptions may produce different interpretations of the same observations.

The application should make relevant assumptions accessible and avoid presenting model-dependent conclusions without their methodological context.

Users should be able to understand whether an interpretation is robust across reasonable assumptions or depends strongly on a particular model configuration.

## 5.4.6 Alternative Interpretations and Competing Explanations

### Core Requirements

- Allow users to consider multiple scientifically plausible interpretations of the same evidence.
- Support comparison of competing hypotheses, models and analytical explanations.
- Preserve the evidence and assumptions associated with each interpretation.
- Identify where interpretations agree or differ.
- Communicate when available observations cannot distinguish between competing explanations.
- Avoid automatically treating one plausible interpretation as uniquely established.
- Allow users to record unresolved questions and alternative explanations.
- Support revision of interpretations when additional evidence becomes available.
- Preserve earlier interpretations and their supporting evidence where relevant.
- Distinguish established alternatives from speculative possibilities.
- Identify relevant observations, reference data or additional analyses that could help distinguish between competing explanations where this can be determined.

### Design Guidance

Scientific evidence does not always support a single unambiguous explanation.

For example, a spectral feature may be consistent with more than one molecular species, particularly when spectral lines overlap or observational resolution is limited.

The application should support comparison and qualification rather than automatically selecting one explanation without sufficient evidence.

It should also communicate when additional evidence would be necessary to distinguish between alternatives.

## 5.4.7 Statistical Results and Interpretation

### Core Requirements

- Present statistical results with appropriate methodological context.
- Identify the statistical methods used to produce reported results.
- Preserve relevant sample sizes, assumptions, parameter estimates and uncertainty information.
- Distinguish statistical significance from physical or scientific importance.
- Identify limitations associated with small samples, incomplete coverage or unsuitable statistical assumptions.
- Communicate relevant effects of selection bias, sampling and data quality.
- Avoid presenting statistical associations as proof of causation.
- Identify when statistical conclusions depend strongly on model or method selection.
- Support inspection of relevant statistical outputs and diagnostics.
- Coordinate detailed statistical and analytical capabilities with Section 3.

### Design Guidance

Statistical outputs require interpretation within the context of the underlying observations and research question.

A statistically significant result is not automatically a physically meaningful discovery, and a weak statistical result does not necessarily establish the absence of a phenomenon.

The application should present statistical findings without overstating what they demonstrate.

## 5.4.8 Negative, Null and Inconclusive Results

### Core Requirements

- Support recording and presentation of negative, null and inconclusive findings.
- Distinguish the absence of detected evidence from evidence that a phenomenon does not exist.
- Identify observational limitations that affect non-detections.
- Preserve relevant detection thresholds, sensitivity limits and uncertainty information.
- Support comparison between detections, non-detections and inconclusive observations.
- Avoid treating unsuccessful detection as proof of absence.
- Allow users to document inconclusive analyses without forcing a definitive conclusion.
- Preserve scientifically useful results even when an investigation does not support its original hypothesis.
- Support appropriate reporting of limitations and unresolved questions.
- Communicate when the available observations or analytical methods are insufficient to distinguish between a genuine absence and a signal below the relevant detection capability.

### Design Guidance

A failure to detect a signal does not necessarily demonstrate that the signal or underlying phenomenon is absent.

For example, a non-detection of an atmospheric molecule may reflect insufficient observational sensitivity, spectral coverage, instrumental limitations or uncertainty in the expected signal.

The application should help users communicate what a non-detection establishes and what remains unresolved.

## 5.4.9 Anomalies, Outliers and Unexpected Findings

### Core Requirements

- Distinguish unusual observations from confirmed errors.
- Preserve anomalous and unexpected measurements for inspection.
- Support investigation of potential instrumental, observational, processing and scientific explanations.
- Identify when an anomaly may be associated with known data-quality concerns.
- Avoid automatically removing unusual results without an appropriate basis.
- Allow users to document suspected causes and alternative explanations.
- Preserve the original evidence associated with an anomaly.
- Support comparison with related observations and independent datasets.
- Communicate when an unexpected result requires additional validation.
- Avoid dismissing unusual results solely because they conflict with expected behavior.

### Design Guidance

Unexpected results may indicate errors, but they may also represent scientifically important discoveries.

For example, an unusual photometric signal could arise from an instrumental artifact, a data-processing problem or a genuine transient astronomical event.

The application should help users investigate these possibilities without prematurely classifying the observation as invalid.

## 5.4.10 Scientific Conclusions and Evidential Qualification

### Core Requirements

- Encourage conclusions that are proportionate to the available evidence.
- Distinguish supported findings from tentative interpretations and speculative explanations.
- Identify important limitations affecting a conclusion.
- Preserve links between conclusions and their supporting observations, analyses and models.
- Avoid presenting inferred properties as confirmed observations.
- Communicate when a conclusion depends on specific assumptions or methodological choices.
- Allow users to revise conclusions as evidence changes.
- Preserve previous conclusions and relevant research context.
- Support appropriate scientific language when presenting uncertainty and evidential limitations.
- Avoid overstating discoveries, detections or scientific implications.
- Identify when further investigation is necessary before a conclusion can be considered sufficiently supported.

### Design Guidance

Scientific conclusions should reflect what the evidence actually supports.

For example, evidence that a planet has conditions potentially compatible with liquid water does not establish that the planet is habitable, and evidence of potentially relevant atmospheric chemistry does not establish the presence of life.

The application should encourage appropriately qualified scientific statements and preserve the distinction between evidence, interpretation and broader implications.

## 5.4.11 Uncertainty in Comparisons and Combined Evidence

### Core Requirements

- Preserve relevant uncertainty when comparing multiple datasets or analytical results.
- Identify differences in measurement conventions, calibration, resolution and observational conditions.
- Distinguish direct comparisons from comparisons requiring transformations or assumptions.
- Communicate when datasets have incompatible or incomplete uncertainty information.
- Support appropriate comparison of independent observations.
- Identify when multiple results share common sources of systematic uncertainty.
- Avoid treating repeated measurements as fully independent when they are not.
- Preserve the origin and uncertainty of combined or summarized results.
- Identify assumptions used when combining evidence from different sources.
- Support inspection of contributing datasets and results.
- Avoid implying that combining multiple results automatically produces a more reliable conclusion when their limitations or dependencies have not been adequately considered.

### Design Guidance

Combining observations can strengthen scientific conclusions, but only when their relationships and limitations are understood.

For example, measurements from different instruments may have different calibration uncertainties, spectral resolutions or observational sensitivities.

The application should preserve these differences and avoid presenting combined results as more certain than the underlying evidence supports.

## 5.4.12 Interpretation in Automated and Potential Future AI-Assisted Workflows

### Core Requirements

- Apply the same evidential and uncertainty standards to automated interpretations as to manually produced interpretations.
- Identify when an interpretation was generated or assisted by an automated system.
- Distinguish automated suggestions from validated scientific results.
- Preserve the evidence and assumptions associated with automated interpretations where available.
- Identify when an automated interpretation relies on incomplete or ambiguous information.
- Avoid presenting automatically generated explanations as established scientific conclusions.
- Allow users to inspect, challenge, modify or reject automated interpretations.
- Support comparison with established scientific references and independent evidence.
- Communicate the limitations of automated interpretation methods.
- If AI-assisted scientific interpretation is ever considered for implementation, apply these same safeguards to its outputs.
- Do not treat AI-assisted scientific interpretation as a required capability, an expected development milestone or part of the initial implementation.
- Coordinate any future consideration of AI-assisted scientific capabilities with Section 5.7.

### Design Guidance

Automated systems may help identify patterns, summarize observations or compare scientific evidence.

However, their outputs must remain subject to appropriate scientific evaluation.

If AI-assisted scientific interpretation is ever investigated as a potential future capability, an AI-generated identification of a possible spectral feature, for example, should be presented as a candidate interpretation until supported by appropriate analysis and evidence.

These requirements establish safeguards for automated interpretation and describe conditions that would apply if AI-assisted scientific capabilities were ever pursued. They do not establish a commitment or requirement to develop or integrate such capabilities.

## 5.4.13 Scientific Presentation of Uncertainty and Limitations

### Core Requirements

- Present uncertainty and limitations alongside the relevant results.
- Use appropriate numerical, graphical and descriptive representations.
- Ensure visualizations do not conceal important uncertainty or imply unsupported precision.
- Distinguish measured values, inferred values, model predictions and estimates in reports and visualizations.
- Preserve relevant caveats when exporting or presenting results.
- Avoid removing uncertainty information from summaries in ways that change scientific meaning.
- Allow users to inspect detailed uncertainty information when appropriate.
- Maintain consistency between numerical results, visual representations and written interpretations.
- Communicate when uncertainty information is unavailable rather than implying certainty.
- Support scientifically appropriate reporting and documentation.
- Ensure that summaries, visualizations and exported results do not communicate stronger conclusions than the underlying evidence supports.

### Design Guidance

Scientific uncertainty should remain visible throughout the research process.

A graph, summary or report should not communicate greater certainty than the underlying analysis supports.

For example, a fitted atmospheric parameter should be presented with its uncertainty and relevant model assumptions rather than as an isolated definitive value.

## 5.4.14 Interactive Investigation and Sensitivity Analysis

### Core Requirements

- Allow users to manually investigate competing scientific interpretations where the relevant analytical methods support this functionality.
- Allow users to adjust relevant assumptions, parameters and model configurations to investigate their effects on results.
- Support comparisons of alternative interpretations, including side-by-side comparisons where appropriate.
- Clearly identify the configurations, assumptions and input conditions associated with each comparison.
- Show how well each interpretation explains the available evidence using scientifically appropriate comparison methods.
- Distinguish user-adjusted scenarios from conclusions supported by the original observations.
- Allow users to return to previous configurations and compare results without losing earlier work.
- Record relevant parameter and assumption changes so that investigations can be revisited and reproduced.
- Identify parameters that significantly influence calculated, fitted or retrieved results where the selected method supports such an assessment.
- Identify parameters with relatively little influence where scientifically appropriate.
- Help users identify where conclusions are particularly sensitive to assumptions or uncertain inputs.
- Support comparisons across different parameter configurations.
- Provide visualizations of parameter sensitivities and their effects on results where scientifically appropriate.
- Preserve the configurations and results of sensitivity investigations so they can be revisited and reproduced.
- Explain the implications of sensitivity findings for interpreting scientific conclusions.
- Avoid presenting exploratory parameter changes as validated scientific results without appropriate evaluation.

### Design Guidance

Users should be able to investigate how reasonable changes to assumptions and model parameters affect scientific interpretations.

For example, when estimating an exoplanet's atmospheric composition, users could investigate how changing temperature, pressure, cloud assumptions or other model parameters affects retrieved molecular abundances.

Sensitivity analysis can help establish whether an interpretation remains reasonably consistent across relevant configurations or depends strongly on particular assumptions.

The application should make these relationships understandable without implying that every parameter adjustment represents an equally scientifically valid model.

Interactive investigation should preserve the distinction between the original analysis and exploratory alternatives. Changes made during investigation should not silently overwrite original observations, established results or previously recorded configurations.

The methods used to preserve and reproduce these investigations should coordinate with the analysis reproducibility requirements in Section 5.5.

## 5.4.15 Interpretation-Specific Acceptance Criteria

Interpretation-related capabilities should not be considered complete simply because their interfaces or basic functionality have been implemented. They must demonstrate scientifically appropriate behavior through testing and validation.

Acceptance criteria should be established for the particular interpretation capability being implemented. Testing should evaluate whether the application represents evidence, uncertainty, assumptions and conclusions correctly under relevant scientific conditions.

### Evidence Classification

- Results can be distinguished according to their evidential status.
- Observations are not incorrectly presented as derived findings, predictions or confirmed conclusions.
- Processed and model-generated results retain their appropriate classifications.
- Evidential classifications remain visible in relevant visualizations, reports and exports.
- Test cases demonstrate that uncertain or tentative findings are not incorrectly represented as confirmed.

### Uncertainty Representation

- Relevant uncertainty information is displayed alongside associated results.
- Uncertainty values are handled and propagated using scientifically appropriate methods for the relevant analysis.
- Missing or unavailable uncertainty information is identified rather than silently treated as zero or ignored.
- The application does not imply unsupported precision or confidence.
- Test cases verify that uncertainty is communicated correctly under different data-quality and analysis conditions.
- Scientific validation establishes that uncertainty calculations and representations are appropriate for the implemented method.

### Interpretation Explanations

- Interpretations can be traced to the observations, datasets, methods and assumptions supporting them.
- Users can inspect the evidence and reasoning behind an interpretation.
- Relevant limitations and potential sources of error are identified.
- Explanations do not claim that evidence proves more than it actually supports.
- Testing includes cases where an interpretation is strongly supported, weakly supported and unsupported.

### Alternative Explanations

- Where scientifically relevant alternatives exist, the application can identify or present them for investigation.
- Competing explanations can be evaluated against the available evidence.
- Relevant overlapping signals, confounding factors and potential artifacts are considered where applicable.
- The application communicates differences in evidential support rather than automatically treating all alternatives equally.
- Where alternatives cannot be distinguished using available data, this limitation is explicitly communicated.
- Testing includes ambiguous cases where multiple interpretations remain plausible.

### Interactive Interpretation Comparisons

- Users can configure and compare alternative interpretations where the relevant scientific method supports this functionality.
- Parameter and assumption changes produce corresponding, scientifically valid updates to results.
- Comparisons clearly identify the configurations and assumptions used.
- Previous configurations and their associated results can be revisited.
- Changes made during exploration do not silently overwrite original observations or previously recorded results.
- Tests verify that comparisons are consistent, repeatable and correctly associated with their configurations.

### Sensitivity Analysis

- Sensitivity analysis uses scientifically appropriate methods for the relevant model or analytical procedure.
- Changes in input parameters produce correctly calculated changes in outputs.
- The application can identify parameters with significant influence where the chosen method supports that conclusion.
- Results clearly communicate the scope and limitations of the sensitivity analysis.
- Sensitivity findings can be reproduced using the recorded configurations and parameters.
- Test cases include known or independently verifiable examples to assess the correctness of sensitivity calculations.

### Insufficient Evidence and Limitations

- The application can communicate when available evidence is insufficient to support a reliable interpretation.
- It does not force a definitive conclusion when the data or methodology cannot justify one.
- Relevant limitations and unresolved uncertainties are communicated.
- Where possible, the application identifies additional observations or analyses that could help resolve uncertainty.
- Tests include incomplete, ambiguous, noisy and otherwise unsuitable datasets to verify that the application handles them appropriately.

### Scientific Validation

- Scientific calculations and interpretation methods are verified against appropriate established methods, reference cases, published results or independently validated implementations.
- Tests cover normal operation, edge cases, ambiguous results and unsuitable inputs.
- Known limitations and unsupported scenarios are documented.
- Scientific correctness is assessed separately from interface functionality.
- A feature is not considered scientifically validated merely because it produces plausible-looking results or passes basic software tests.
- Acceptance criteria reflect the scientific purpose and limitations of the particular capability being implemented.

### Acceptance Criteria Scope

Acceptance criteria should be adapted to the scientific capability being implemented. Not every interpretation feature will apply to every scientific discipline or analytical workflow.

For example, sensitivity analysis may be relevant to a model-based retrieval workflow but not to a simple observational measurement display. Alternative interpretation comparisons may be essential for ambiguous spectral identification but unnecessary for some straightforward data presentation functions.

The application should use scientifically appropriate validation methods for each specific capability rather than requiring every listed interpretation feature to be implemented universally.

## General Principles

Scientific interpretation and uncertainty requirements should:

- Preserve the distinction between observations, measurements, derived results, inferences, predictions and hypotheses.
- Treat uncertainty as an essential component of scientific information.
- Distinguish measurement uncertainty from broader scientific confidence.
- Preserve relevant statistical, systematic, instrumental and model-related limitations.
- Make methodological assumptions and limitations accessible.
- Support competing interpretations and alternative explanations.
- Allow users to investigate how relevant assumptions and parameters affect results.
- Support sensitivity analysis where scientifically appropriate.
- Preserve negative, null and inconclusive results.
- Avoid treating anomalies as automatic errors.
- Encourage conclusions proportionate to the available evidence.
- Apply consistent evidential standards to manual and automated interpretations.
- Apply the same safeguards to AI-assisted interpretations if such capabilities are ever introduced.
- Communicate uncertainty and limitations in scientific outputs and presentations.
- Require scientific validation appropriate to each implemented interpretation capability.

## General Principle

Scientific interpretation should never communicate greater certainty than the available evidence justifies.

The application should help users understand not only what the data may indicate, but also how confidently it can be interpreted, what assumptions are involved and what remains uncertain.

The objective is not merely to generate conclusions, but to help users investigate, evaluate and understand how strongly those conclusions are supported and what could change them.

## Implementation Boundary

This subsection establishes requirements for scientific interpretation, uncertainty representation, evidential strength, competing explanations, interactive investigation, sensitivity analysis and the communication and validation of scientific conclusions.

It does not prescribe specific statistical algorithms, uncertainty-propagation methods, confidence-scoring systems, model-selection techniques, sensitivity-analysis algorithms or AI implementation technologies.

Scientific integrity is established in Section 5.1. Automation and user control are addressed in Section 5.2. Data processing safeguards are addressed in Section 5.3. Analysis reproducibility and traceability are addressed in Section 5.5. Validation and quality control are addressed in Section 5.6. AI-assisted scientific analysis and interpretation, as a distant future possibility rather than a required capability, are addressed in Section 5.7.
