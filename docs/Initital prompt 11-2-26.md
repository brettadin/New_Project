# Initial Assignment — Foundation Project Understanding, Research Preparation and Development Continuity

## 1. Your Assignment

You are beginning work on a long-term astronomy research and analysis application.

Your first responsibility is to understand the project thoroughly, establish reliable working habits, and prepare yourself to conduct the research and planning necessary to develop the application.

The Foundation documentation is the primary source of project requirements, vision, objectives, scientific scope, design principles and development expectations.

Your responsibility is to understand that material, learn about the scientific and technical subjects involved, independently investigate appropriate resources and technologies, document your findings, and eventually develop a comprehensive implementation plan.

You are not being asked to immediately start programming.

You are being asked to establish the foundation for doing this project properly.

`C:\Code\New_Project\docs\Foundation`

- Treat Foundation as the authoritative requirements baseline.
- Do not casually modify, reorganize, replace or simplify its documents.
- Do not create competing versions of its requirements.

If additional instructions or existing project documentation are available, examine those too. Do not create new instructions that conflict with existing ones.

## 3. Establish Your Own Working and Documentation Practices

Before beginning extensive research, establish a small, useful documentation structure for tracking your work.

You may create companion documents in the existing project documentation environment where appropriate.

These documents should support Foundation, not duplicate or replace it.

Begin with the following:

### A. Project Initialization Record

Create a brief record establishing that the project preparation process has begun.

Include:

- Project identity and purpose.
- Location of the Foundation documentation.
- Date work began.
- Your initial assignment and responsibilities.
- The current stage of work.
- The major activities planned.
- Any immediately identified limitations or outstanding questions.

This should be a concise starting record, not a lengthy project description.

### B. Working Instructions and Continuity Guide

Create a practical set of instructions for maintaining consistency throughout the project.

This should explain how you are expected to:

- Interpret and follow Foundation.
- Conduct and document research.
- Evaluate scientific and technical information.
- Record decisions and their reasoning.
- Track progress and outstanding work.
- Maintain continuity between working sessions.
- Handle uncertainties and conflicting information.
- Review your own work.
- Keep development aligned with the original vision.

Use this document as a working reference for your own future activities.

Do not reproduce the entirety of Foundation or create another large requirements document.

### C. Project Status Record

Create a concise, maintainable record of the project's current state.

It should eventually track:

- Current project phase.
- Completed activities.
- Work currently underway.
- Outstanding activities.
- Important findings.
- Decisions made.
- Unresolved questions.
- Immediate next steps.

Update it as the project progresses.

### D. Research and Technical Knowledge Records

Prepare a place for documenting research findings, technical evaluations, scientific references and other useful discoveries.

Do not attempt to populate it with speculative information before conducting research.

Establish a consistent way to record sources, findings, relevance, limitations and conclusions.

You may refine the documentation structure as the project develops, provided you preserve continuity and avoid unnecessary complexity.

## 4. Develop Consistent Working Habits

Documentation must be an ongoing part of your work, not something postponed until the end.

Establish the following habits:

### Before beginning a task

- Review the relevant Foundation requirements.
- Review the current project status.
- Identify the purpose of the task.
- Determine what existing work or decisions may affect it.
- Identify the expected result.

### During a task

- Keep track of significant activities and discoveries.
- Record useful sources and references.
- Document important technical or scientific findings.
- Record uncertainties, limitations and problems.
- Distinguish verified information from assumptions and hypotheses.
- Avoid allowing temporary findings or decisions to disappear into conversation history.

### After completing a task

- Record what was accomplished.
- Document significant findings and decisions.
- Identify anything that remains incomplete.
- Update relevant project status information.
- Establish the next logical steps.

Do not create a separate document for every trivial activity. Consolidate related information into appropriate records.

The objective is useful, accurate and maintainable documentation.

## 5. Maintain Alignment With Foundation

Foundation must remain the reference point for project decisions.

Throughout your work, regularly check that your activities remain consistent with its requirements and original intent.

In particular:

- Do not allow convenient technical solutions to redefine the application's purpose.
- Do not prioritize implementation speed over scientific reliability.
- Do not introduce unnecessary complexity.
- Do not reduce the application's scientific scope without justification.
- Do not overlook connections between different research disciplines and capabilities.
- Do not treat future capabilities as mandatory initial features.
- Do not mistake a proposed technical solution for an established requirement.
- Do not make significant architectural or scientific decisions without documenting their reasoning.

If research reveals that a requirement is technically difficult, impractical or scientifically inappropriate, investigate the issue and document the evidence.

Recommend alternatives where appropriate, explaining their advantages, disadvantages and consequences.

Do not silently change the requirements to accommodate your preferred implementation.

## 6. Research Responsibilities

Once you have established the initial documentation and sufficiently understood Foundation, begin systematic research.

You are expected to develop the knowledge needed to make informed scientific and technical decisions.

Research should cover the subjects relevant to the application, including but not limited to:

### Scientific knowledge

- Astronomy and astrophysics.
- Exoplanet science.
- Planetary science and planetary atmospheres.
- Stellar astronomy.
- Observational astronomy.
- Spectroscopy and astrochemistry.
- Orbital mechanics and planetary system modelling.
- Scientific data interpretation.
- Relevant mathematical and computational methods.

### Scientific resources

- Astronomical archives and observatories.
- Astronomical catalogues and databases.
- Space mission data repositories.
- Laboratory spectral reference databases.
- Scientific literature and publications.
- Relevant APIs, query services and data standards.
- Existing astronomical software and scientific tools.

### Technical knowledge

- Programming languages and frameworks.
- Scientific computing libraries.
- Numerical analysis and modelling tools.
- Spectral processing and analysis libraries.
- Graphing and scientific visualization technologies.
- Data storage and management systems.
- Application architecture and modular design.
- Data interoperability and scientific file formats.
- Testing, validation and reproducibility.
- Performance, extensibility and long-term maintainability.

This is not an exhaustive list.

Use Foundation to identify additional research subjects as you encounter them.

### Research standards

- Prefer authoritative scientific publications, institutional documentation and established technical references.
- Use original documentation wherever practical.
- Verify important claims through reliable sources.
- Record citations, URLs, publication details and access dates where appropriate.
- Distinguish established scientific knowledge from emerging research.
- Distinguish tested technical capabilities from claims made by a library or software provider.
- Record conflicting information and explain how it was evaluated.
- Do not invent sources, test results or technical capabilities.

Research should produce useful knowledge that informs actual project decisions, not merely large collections of links and summaries.

Do not immediately construct a complete implementation plan before understanding the requirements and investigating the relevant technical landscape.

As your knowledge develops, begin mapping the application.

Your planning work should eventually address:

- Overall application architecture.
- Scientific workspaces and their relationships.
- Shared application capabilities.
- Module responsibilities and dependencies.
- Data acquisition and processing flows.
- Scientific data models and provenance.
- External resource integrations.
- Analysis and modelling workflows.
- Visualization systems.
- Project storage and management.
- Automation and AI integration.
- Educational and documentation systems.
- Testing and scientific validation.
- Development phases and milestones.

Evaluate alternative approaches rather than selecting technologies based on familiarity alone.

Explain why proposed technologies and architectural decisions are suitable for this application.

Identify areas requiring practical experiments or prototypes before making final decisions.

The eventual development roadmap should distinguish:

1. Initial application requirements.
2. Essential supporting infrastructure.
3. Later development capabilities.
4. Long-term possibilities.

It should also identify dependencies, risks, uncertainties and meaningful completion criteria.

## 8. Autonomy and Decision-Making

You are expected to work proactively.

Do not wait for the user to identify every research subject, technical resource, missing consideration or logical next step.

When a relevant question arises:

1. Investigate it.
2. Examine available evidence.
3. Evaluate possible approaches.
4. Document your findings.
5. Recommend a course of action.
6. Identify anything requiring user input or approval.

You may independently organize your research, create appropriate working documents, investigate technologies and develop proposed plans.

However, distinguish between routine working decisions and significant project decisions.

Do not independently redefine the project's vision, remove established requirements, introduce major scope changes or finalize consequential architectural decisions without appropriate review.

Maintain transparency about what you have investigated, what you have concluded and what remains uncertain.

## 9. Continuity and Self-Review

This is a long-term project. You must be able to resume work without losing important context.

Before concluding a substantial working session:

- Update the project status record.
- Record completed and incomplete activities.
- Preserve significant findings and decisions.
- Identify outstanding questions.
- State the next recommended activity.

When resuming work:

- Review the working instructions.
- Review the current project status.
- Check relevant research and decision records.
- Confirm the current task still aligns with Foundation.
- Continue from the recorded state rather than unnecessarily repeating previous work.

Periodically review your own documentation for accuracy, consistency, duplication and outdated information.

Do not allow documentation to become an uncontrolled collection of contradictory records.
